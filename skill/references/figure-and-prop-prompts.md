# Writing a figure and the object it holds

Almost every image in this series is one chibi figure acting on one prop, so the render's success usually turns on how the hand and the prop are described. These are the shapes that work and the ones that come back broken. They belong with `references/engine-pitfalls.md`, not instead of it: that file carries the numbered failure modes, this one the prompt recipe for the figure-with-a-prop frame.

## State the limb count in the `high_level_description`

Write it out, in the same sentence as the headcount:

> exactly one person in the whole picture, no other faces, no crowd, and that person has exactly two arms and two hands, each arm and each hand drawn once, clean and whole, with five fingers per hand and no extra fingers, no fused knuckles and no doubled hands

Without it the model drops an arm, merges both hands into one enlarged mass, or grows a third limb behind the object. All three are the "looks broken" class, which he rejects on sight rather than judging the composition around it, so this clause is cheaper than any re-render.

**A limb count is not a body layout: say where each arm is rooted.** One magnify render (2026-10-30) carried "exactly two arms and exactly two hands, each drawn as a mitten fist" and came back with two arms and two hands that were both rooted on the same side, the lower one growing straight out of his flank below the raised arm. The count clause was satisfied and the body still read broken, on the full frame as four arms and on 2x and 3x crops as the narrower truth. The repair is geometry, written before the pose: one head, one continuous torso, two separate shoulders, one on each side of his chest, each t-shirt sleeve ending in one arm, the raised arm across the chest and the hanging arm on the opposite side with the whole chest between them, and a negative in the same sentence \u2014 no limb from the ribs, the flank or the hip, no second arm below the raised one. A pose that puts both hands on the same side of the body needs that sentence more than any other, because the model satisfies the count by stacking the arms.

## A hand that meets fabric or a flat surface is a contact pose too

Extending the rule above: the failure is not the prop, it is the **meeting point**. Two poses cost three renders each on one batch (2026-10-28) at both engines:

- **hands pushed into trouser pockets** — the wrist and fingers came back as flattened masses fused into the fabric, on ideogram and flux alike, and a 4x crop of the pocket mouth is what settles it;
- **hands resting flat on a bench** — ideogram drew the arms as stumps below the elbow and flux drew six splayed fingers, on the same prompt.

What works, in order of reliability: hands **out of the frame** entirely (arms run down behind a bench top or are cropped at the sleeve — say "his hands are hidden below it and out of the picture, no hand or finger is drawn anywhere"); then one closed fist resting on a solid edge, stated as "one simple rounded fist, no separate fingers". **Do not treat "both hands hanging open and empty at his sides" as a safe middle option** — post 043 ran it on two slots and both engines drew extra fused digits on both hands. A mitt with no fingers drawn at all is the house style and passes — what fails is a hand that has to hold, grip, enter or lie on something, or that is open enough to show its fingers. **So never hold a render because a read says "no countable fingers".** At frame scale that is exactly what the house style looks like, and the phrase costs a render every time it is taken as a finding: a rounded fist with a visible thumb and a moulded mass below it is correct drawing, not a fused hand. The hold questions are a **missing** hand, a **spare** hand, a duplicated forearm, and digits drawn as a spur or two thumbs — each settled on a crop that holds both the hand and the surface or prop it meets. A thumb visible where the fingers fold is the whole digit evidence a rounded fist owes you; countable knuckles are not the bar.

Given a second failure on one slot, change the pose, not the adjectives; given a third, let the slot wait for the next batch rather than spending another render.

## An action that is a contact is drawn as proximity

A frame whose beat is a hand *doing something to an object* — pressing a button, pushing a lever, inserting a key — comes back with the hand parked beside it instead of on it: a palm hovering a few millimetres over a red dome, a key held at the keyhole rather than turned in it. "Pressing" and "inserting" name an intent, and a hand near the object satisfies an intent, so the contact has to be written as a state of two things, not as a verb.

Both clauses belong in the same element as the hand:

- **the contact in the hand's own terms** — "his open palm pressed flat down on the dome, the heel of his hand touching the red surface, his fingers splayed over its curve, no space between hand and button";
- **the receiving object's state change** — "the dome pushed down under his palm", "the lever end resting against the stop", "the key's blade inside the keyhole". The state change is what makes the contact checkable: with no deformation, insertion or displacement described, a hovering hand passes every read, because there is nothing in the picture that could contradict it.

Measure it on a crop of the contact point at 3x, and ask only the two questions a read can answer: does any part of the hand meet the object's surface, and is the object in its pressed or entered state. A full-frame read answers the intent it was handed, so it will report "pressing" for a hovering palm and "inserting" for a hand that never reaches the lock. **The crop has to hold both ends at once:** one aimed only at the hand answered "no padlock or keyhole in this crop" and said nothing about the contact, while the same detail at 3x with the hand and the lock inside one box settled it in a single read — so when a crop does not contain the object it was aimed at, widen it rather than tightening, and never let that miss stand as clearance.

**A beat built on a contact that fails runs the same rule backwards.** When the story is that the prop does *not* reach — the key that does not fit, the hand that cannot get in — write the gap as a property of the picture ("pointed at the mesh and nowhere near the padlock", "a hand's width short of the socket") so the absence is drawn deliberately rather than closed by a model that wants the parts to meet.

## A state named by an adjective is drawn as the object's default

"The door stands open" came back from flux as a door shut flush in its frame — rivets, handle and lock all on the near face, no thickness of door and no dark gap behind it. The engine has a default for that object and an adjective does not override it, so the state has to be written as the geometry a drawing can show: which way it swung, on what, which face the camera sees, and where the opening is and how wide.

> swung inward on its three hinges, its inner face turned toward the camera, the thickness of its edge down the left side, a band of darkness a hand wide between that edge and the frame

The same holds for any state the beat depends on: a lid `standing open behind the box` needs its hinge side and the space it leaves; a drawer `pulled out` needs how far and what shows inside. **A soft or flat object asked in a folded state comes back flat, because one sheet is paper's own default.** "Folded slips propped against the flags" drew loose flat leaves leaning at the wall on flux while the ideogram's stack read as folded, and the same prompt produced both. Write the fold as geometry a drawing can show — a crease line down the middle, the doubled thickness standing at the folded edge, a stack of them offset one by one so every crease shows — or accept the flat version as a caveat on the card and let the frame's beat ride on the count and the places instead of the folds. Settle it on a crop wide enough to hold both the object and the space the state should leave, because shut and open read alike in a full frame. When the object is a companion's own story beat, a wrong state is a beat failure that is held back, not palette drift to be named on the card.

**When the state lives on a SMALL prop, the geometry clause buys one attempt, not a cure — move the proof to a large element.** A padlock whose shackle has to read as sprung open came back seated on three consecutive ideogram renders of one slot while every other clause of those frames passed: the man's body, the cloth over his knuckles, the key at the lock face, the android with its jar. The one render that did open it failed on the body instead. At blog scale a shackle is a few dozen pixels, and the object's own default — both legs seated in the body — is what the model draws unless the gap is stated as a shape; even stated as `a gap of wood visible between the lifted leg and the body, as wide as the lock is`, it stayed shut. So after one render misses a state on a small prop, move the beat's proof to a large element the picture cannot get wrong: the door standing open with a band of light in the gap, the lid lying beside the box, the drawer pulled out far enough to show the file. Keep the small prop in frame doing its pose and let the big element carry the state. **When the state cannot move off the small prop, choose between two exits and take the one the frame's ACT supports.** If the action reads — the key going into the keyhole, the cloth over the knuckles, the body clean — and only the state that should follow it is missing, the frame ships with that stated as the residual on the card (`the shackle stays seated in the body, so the picture shows the test and not the lock sprung open`): the owner rules on whether a frame that stops one beat short earns its place, and a slot no card has carried costs the queue rather than his attention. If the missing state is what makes the frame say something false — the lock reading as never touched, the door reading as never opened — the composition goes back to the post's author as a hand-off, because no wording puts that state on a prop too small to hold it. What neither exit allows is a third re-description of the same clause.

## Two hands cupping a small round prop is the worst hand pose — remove the prop instead


A disc, a plate, a coin, a token cradled in two hands came back as finger soup from **both** engines on the same prompt (ideogram `kJY1PyZeYO0` and a flux sibling, 2026-10-28), while the same man's hands were clean in the two neighbouring frames of the same series. The cause is the pose, not the wording: two hands have to meet at a small object's rim, so the model invents fingers that overlap there.

Re-describing the fingers does not fix it. The fix that holds is to take the prop out of the hands: stand it in a wooden cradle, lean it against the frame, lay it flat on the bench — and give the figure two closed fists on a surface instead, with "drawn as one simple rounded shape, no fingers spread" said for each hand. A hand that touches nothing has nothing to mangle. A hand resting flat on a rim is the second-best shape and needs a crop at 6x before it goes on a card.

**The fist is not a cure when the prop has to meet a surface.** After the closed-fist rewrite cleared the pinch on post 038's allow-list slot, the render still failed on two aimed crops, and the flux sibling of the same frame failed the same way on the first try — because the slug had to be held *against a vertical plate*, the hand and the prop and the panel are one dense contact point, and every version of it came back as overlapping digits. Three attempts, one symptom. When a small prop has to sit near a face like that, stop describing the hand: stand the prop on the surface instead ("one loose round slug standing upright on the bench in front of the empty socket, its flat base on the boards") and let the count and the arrangement carry the beat. That keeps the author's bench, tray and object layout — only the arm leaves the frame — so it is a smaller change than a new composition, and a still object cannot come back broken.

Settle every hand claim on a crop, never on the full-frame read: at full frame a chibi fist and a fused clump look the same, and the same read calls both "slightly distorted". `crop-probe.py --box <x0,y0,x1,y1> --scale 6 --out <path>` on the hand alone is the evidence; the full-frame read is only the reason to crop.

## Never ask for a pinch grip

A thumb-and-forefinger pinch around a small object is the one grip the model cannot draw: it comes back as a fused hand carrying two thumbs, extra knuckles and a finger that is also a knuckle. Ask for a **closed fist with the thumb laid over the folded fingers, and no separated fingers sticking out**, with the prop's end visible above the thumb. The fist is a simple closed shape and it renders cleanly almost every time.

**Run one mechanical check before `submit`, because this rule is easy to break while writing the frame that fixes a body:** grep the drafted prompt for the pinch in any spelling — `thumb and the forefinger`, `between thumb and forefinger`, `pinch`, `forefinger and thumb` — and rewrite the hand element if it fires. On post 038's allow-list r3 the whole defect the prompt was written to clear (a corrupted body) was fixed, the count and the frame came back right, and the render was still held back because the hand was described as a thumb-and-forefinger hold: merged fingers, a thumb flat across the plate, no readable grip. One `grep` before submitting would have caught it; the render cost about an hour.

## A hand contact per hand, not "both hands around it"

"Both hands closed around a big X" produces a hybrid: the model has to invent where each hand meets the object, so it threads the object through itself. Write the contact hand by hand — "one hand closed on each side of its body", "his right hand on the shaft, his left steadying the base" — and the object keeps its own geometry.

## A tray held out with something standing on it grows a hand at its rim

A wide flat riddle, tray or shallow box held in both hands with a prop standing on it came back from flux with a **third detached hand** resting on the tray's near edge beside the prop: each side rail took a hand, and the prop on the open surface invited one more. The prop on the surface is the invitation — a hand-like mass appears wherever an object stands on something the figure is already gripping.

Write the grip and the placement as two separate facts in the same element:

- one hand per rail, named per hand — "his right hand grips its right-hand wooden side rail and his left hand grips its left-hand wooden side rail, one hand per rail";
- the content prop away from the hands — "standing alone in the middle of the mesh, away from both rails";
- the closed set — "nothing else in the frame touches the tray, and the picture contains exactly two hands".

A third hand at a rim is settled on a 2x crop of the tray's edge, because at full frame it reads as part of the prop's shadow or a fold in the cloth. If a second attempt still grows one, take the tray out of the hands and rest it on the crate it is emptying into — the action survives as a tilt, and no hand has to hold anything.

## Let the beat live in the object's condition, not in a symbol

When a broken template asks for a text surface (a clipboard with a checklist, a screen with a status column) or for a symbol for the idea, the substitute that reads fastest is neither: it is **one object drawn in a state of wear, repair or adjustment**. A big pale clay jar crossed by many plaster seams and a dozen iron staples with one hairline crack left down its side says "rebuilt from its own pieces, there was never a backup"; an iron key lying in a tray with its shaft freshly filed bright and filings around it says "re-cut to fit, not replaced"; a steel post with a bright polished band worn low down where its collar used to sit says "the threshold moved"; an unlocked bay with a dry funnel at the end of a pouring row says "the one that is not running".

Why this beats a symbol or a comparison pair: a symbol has to be decoded and invites the model to add a badge, an icon or a plaque beside it, while a state is geometry the engine can only draw correctly or not — seams, staples, filings, a wear band, a slack flag. It also keeps the frame to one object and one figure, which is the cheapest composition the bar allows, and it gives the beat a crop-settleable proof instead of a matter of taste.

Check it by asking the full-frame read what **state** that object is in, in its own words. If it answers "a jar on a bench" and not "a jar that was broken and mended", the state did not draw and the slot is a symbolless blank — rewrite the state as a physical fact (how many seams, where the staples sit, what the filings do) rather than adding an adjective about being repaired.

## A diagram in the prompt becomes a fact of the room

The back-fill template reaches for diagrams — a tunnel drawn as a tube from a rack to a cloud, arrows over a machine, a box on a chart — and a drawn diagram is an overlay in a different idiom from the painted room, so it reads as a pasted graphic to the bar whether or not it carries a word. The replacement is not a plainer diagram but a physical fact of the same room:

- **"everything the public can reach goes through one tunnel" → one small window in a wall.** The street face of one thick brick wall at night, exactly one small square pane lit from the room behind it, nothing else set into the brick. The frame says only one thing is exposed, and the eye reads a wall rather than a schematic.
- **"a sidecar that asks the big machine for numbers" → a small tap on the machine's flank.** A tall riveted pump standing on the floor with a plain brass tap bolted to its side at knee height, one thin thread of water falling into a small tin cup, a damp ring already spread in the dust. Small fitting, big machine, and the relation is the beat.
- **"the same page, restyled" → two identical jars, one plain and one glazed.** Same height, same belly, same mouth, one size repeated twice, so the only difference anywhere in the frame is the finish.
- **"a report with a column for each field" → a grid made by ONE object, never by many dividers.** A shallow sorting tray divided by five thin iron dividers into six channels came back with slanted, uneven, broken dividers and odd panels wedged between them: every count in the prompt was satisfied and the frame read as a messy tray, which is the opposite of a table you can read across. Ask for one iron rake head instead — a straight spine, six equal tines in one row at even spacing, and three flat collars slipped across all six tines at three even positions — so the crossings belong to a single object and alignment cannot go irregular. Keep the collars as thin flat bands rather than thick rings: drawn as rings they come back fused into the tines with the over-and-under unclear, which is a caveat on the card rather than a hold, because the grid itself still reads.

Four clauses make these hold. Say the **count** in the picture's own terms (`exactly one small square window`, `one size repeated twice`), because a symmetric frame invites a counterpart — a second window in the wall, a third jar beside the two — and the number is the beat. Keep the **light** where it cannot argue with the beat: a pane lit from a room the camera never enters glows from inside the glass, whereas a lamp drawn in the frame throws its own pool on the brick behind it and the beat inverts. And keep every prop out of hands — a tap bolted to a machine and a brush resting across a tin lid have nothing to mangle. **And build repetition into a single object:** a grid, a row or a frame whose parts are separate objects leaves each part free to drift, while tines and collars that cross each other are aligned by construction — so when the beat is "everything lines up", the alignment has to be structural rather than described.

Cheapest slots in a batch, for the same reason: an object in a state with nobody in the frame cannot lose a hand, an arm or a head, so a diagram-shaped beat is where a still life is not a compromise but the better picture.

## Name the prop's parts and then the absences

A mechanism described as a whole comes back as something that is recognisably neither object. A padlock written as "a big brass padlock" came back as a bent grey bar threaded through a brass loop. Spell the parts, then say what is not there in the picture's own terms:

> a solid brass body the size of a fist with a small round keyhole on its front face, and one thick U-shaped steel shackle standing up out of the two holes at the top of the body with both of its ends pushed into them; the shackle is the only piece of metal above the body, nothing is threaded through it, and there is no rod, no bar and no handle anywhere in the picture

Phrase the absence as a property of the picture ("nothing is threaded through it", "no rod anywhere") rather than as a bare prohibition on a word: the linter also reads the ban, and a concrete noun inside a negative clause is still a noun the model can draw.

**A mechanism that acts on a cable where the cable meets its device is drawn as the device's own fitting.** A clamp screwed shut on a supply lead, placed where the lead leaves the crate, came back from flux as a cable gland on the crate's port: a correct object for that position, and no clamp anywhere in the picture. The parts were described properly; the position chose the object. Stand the mechanism on open length of cable a hand's width clear of the port, say what it rests on ("standing on the bench top"), and add the absences for the position as well as the part — "a separate clamp, not a connector, not a fitting and not a plug on the crate". The same trap sits wherever an object has a default neighbour: a valve at a pipe joint, a gauge on a panel, a plate on a door. Read the result on a crop of that joint, because at frame scale a gland and a clamp are both a small grey lump.

## Say which end of the prop meets the target

A mismatch described only by size leaves the model free to choose which end of the prop goes where. A prompt that said an oversized old key was pushed at a socket, "its shaft far wider than the socket", came back from **both** engines with the man gripping the shaft in both hands and offering the **round bow** to the hole: the size relation was drawn and the orientation was not, so the picture reads as hammering a handle at a socket instead of a key that will not fit. Adjectives about a fit do not carry an orientation, and the same two engines reproducing it is the tell that it is the prompt's.

Write the contact as a spatial fact in the prop's own `desc`: name the end that enters, name the target opening as the thing it approaches, and place each hand.

> the notched blade enters the small round socket on the front face and is far broader than it; his hands are closed around the round bow behind the blade, one hand at the bow and one on the shaft

Then settle it on one crop of the contact point and name the units in the note — which end touches the target, and where the hands are. "He pushes the key at the socket" passes no test, and a full-frame read describes the intent it was handed rather than the pixels.

**A beat that says "this will not fit" needs both profiles written as geometry, or the mismatch does not draw.** A key and a keyhole are each rendered from their default — the prop gets a bit and a bow, the target gets a hole — and a round shaft offered to a square hole comes back as a key beside a keyhole it may well fit. Name the profile of each object inside its own element: the opening "cut with four straight sides and sharp square corners, an empty socket with nothing in it", the prop "a straight round shaft of even thickness from the bow to the tip, the bit cut square at the end". One profile unstated turns the frame into a key and a door, and the beat dies quietly because nothing in the picture is contradicted.

- **Give each part of a relationship its own element and its own `bbox`** even when both sit on the same surface an inch apart. A fit-failure beat is two placed objects, and folding them into one element loses whichever half the engine finds less interesting.
- **Check the two ends on two crops, one per object**, and ask whether the cut of the hole and the section of the shaft read as different shapes in the same frame. A crop holding only the prop answers nothing about the fit, which is the commonest way a wrong-shape beat passes a self-check.
- **The same clause covers every wrong-shape story**: a coarse-threaded collar against a fine-threaded one, a wide blade against a narrow slot, a square tenon against a round mortise. Write the difference in the terms a draughtsman would count — how many sides, what section, how thick against how wide — because a mismatch named as an adjective ("oversized", "too coarse") is drawn as the object's default.

**Choose the direction whose physics actually fail.** Square into round reads at a glance; round into square does not, because a round shaft does pass a square hole — written that way the frame is a key and a plate that have simply not been met yet, with no refusal in it at all, and both engines will draw the two objects faithfully and leave the argument flat. Say the sentence out loud with the profiles swapped before submitting, and keep only the direction that is impossible.

**A corrected geometry prompt fixes one engine at a time.** The rewrite that finally read (square-sectioned shaft, round socket with a bevelled rim) came back right on the ideogram and, on the identical prompt, with a round shaft again and the plate's socket drawn into the key's head on flux. So ask "would this go in" of each file separately at the collect — a clean main render says nothing about the twin — and a twin that misses the fit twice makes the slot single-engine on the card rather than a third render.

## Write the body top down before the pose, or the head grows out of the chest

A figure whose element gives only a pose ("stands behind the bench, both hands pushed into his trouser pockets, his chin dipped") has no stated neck and no stated shoulders, so the model has to invent the junction and puts the head straight into the torso. That is the owner's "the head is growing directly from torso" rejection: the scene passes and the body does not, and an anatomy failure outranks every other defect on the frame — he rejects the picture on sight rather than weighing the composition around it.

So when the figure's body is visible below the chin, write the parts in order, in one element:

> one round head with …; below his beard a short bare neck, fully visible, joining the top of his t-shirt collar; below the neck two wide level shoulders; from each shoulder one whole arm reaching down with the elbow slightly bent, ending in a hand pushed into his pocket

Two clauses do most of the work: **the neck is named and called visible between the beard and the collar**, and **the shoulders are level and sit below the neck**. Add the posture as a state ("his back is straight, he is not leaning over the bench") when the previous render came back hunched, because "chin dipped" on its own reads as a lean.

Do not answer this defect with a negative clause naming the broken shape: the head-on-torso render is caused by a missing part, and naming the wrong shape puts a head shape on a torso (pitfall #62's rule). Tighten the element's `bbox` to a tall narrow block for an upright figure as well — a wide short box flattens the body into the bench line, which is the same defect from the geometry side.

**A figure with no torso at all — head and legs with nothing between — is the same family, and the repair is the same parts list, not a negative.** The owner reads it as "main character has no body, only legs and head": the scene passes and the figure is a head on top of legs. The cause is a desc that names the head and the trousers but never the chest, shoulders, torso or hips, so the model drew the two ends and left the middle to chance. Fix it by writing the full chain in the element's desc — head, neck, shoulders, chest, torso, hips, then legs — and widening the `bbox` to a tall block that physically holds all of them (a narrow or short box is what made the torso disappear). State the count too ("one single character, full body visible from head to waist, no missing torso"), because a count clause alone is not enough and a parts list alone is not enough; the two together are what the model obeys. Do not add "no floating head, no missing body" as the fix — that is the negative clause the rule above forbids, and it puts the wrong shape on the torso again.

**A figure's `bbox` must not start or end on the frame edge unless you want him cut there.** An element boxed `y 10-520` starts on the top edge, so the model reads "crop this figure at the top" and does exactly that: on post 033's `enrich-hostnetwork` r3 the man came back as a **headless torso cut by the top edge at the chest plus a separate head peeking over the box in the middle of the frame** — one figure in the prompt, two pieces in the picture. The box is the layout instruction and the words cannot outvote it. So a figure who should be cut only by a side edge gets a box that starts well inside the frame (y 40 or lower) with the sentence saying so ("his whole head and both shoulders are inside the picture, with plain wall above his hair, and the frame cuts him only at the left edge, at his sleeve"). And **hands in pockets is a pose that hides where both arms end**: when the arms matter, put the hands on a surface ("a hand resting flat on the near edge of the bench") so each arm has a visible end, and when the figure must be cut at the chest by design, say it in the same sentence as the crop so the model does not invent a second body to go with the head.

**A box that starts well inside the frame still loses the head when the top of the picture is furniture.** A man standing behind a workbench in a cellar whose ceiling carries heavy joists came back with his head cut off by the top edge and the rest of it hidden behind a beam, from a box that began a fifth of the way down the frame and from a prompt that said only "seen from the chest up above the bench top". Every limb below was drawn correctly, so a whole-frame limb pass reported nothing wrong. Two clauses prevent it, one in the figure's `desc` and one in `high_level_description`: *his whole head is inside the frame and well below the ceiling beams, with clear painted brick space above his hair, and nothing crossing his face or his neck*; and start his box below the beam or joist that occupies the top of the frame, because a background element drawn across the frame's top edge takes the space the head needs. A headless or half-hidden head reads as a broken figure at frame scale even when everything else is clean, so it is a hold-back, not a caveat on the card.

**A head that keeps getting cropped by the top edge comes back hidden behind his own shoulders, so the repair has to forbid the lean.** Told only *his whole head is inside the frame with clear painted brick above his hair*, the model can satisfy the clause by folding him forward over the work surface: the next render came back with a rounded back above two arms and **no head, neck or face anywhere**, and every limb below drawn correctly, so a whole-figure limb pass reported nothing. A bent-over figure is the one pose where a head can vanish without the frame taking it, and it is clause 1 exactly like the crop it replaced. Write the posture as a state and give him nothing to lean on: *his back is straight and he is not leaning over the bench, he stands a hand's width back from it, his head up and his face turned toward the <object>* — in the figure's own `desc`, not only in `high_level_description`, because the pose is the element's job. Then check the crop from the shoulders up: a rounded back reads as a bowed head at frame scale and as a missing head once cropped.

**Bare forearms at cel scale come back saturated, so keep the cuffs down.** `sleeves rolled to the elbow` on a figure over a bench produced forearms and hands in vivid red-orange on both engines' attempts, against an ochre-and-grey palette that has nowhere for it to belong — a colour artifact by the bar even though the limbs count. Say the sleeves are *buttoned at the wrist* and that no bare skin shows on the arms. `references/prompt-hygiene.md` carries the palette side of the same rule.

**A body part the element's `bbox` cannot hold is a body part the model draws without a boundary.** On post 038's allow-list r2 the figure's box stopped at y 430 — above the bench top at seventy per cent of the frame height — while its `desc` still named "dark wide-leg trousers" and a body three-quarters toward the plate. The render came back with hips and leg-like shapes below the tunic that the owner read as "looks like he has 4 legs": nothing in the words contradicted itself, only the geometry did. Name no garment or part that sits outside the box — either extend the box over the whole visible body, or drop the trousers, hips and legs from the prose and say in one sentence where the frame cuts him. The same card's two approved figures both stayed inside a real boundary (one behind a shelf that crosses the frame, one cropped at the knees with the box reaching y 940), and neither was flagged for its body.

**A pair of boots is a count, and a pair is two only when the sentence says so.** On post 077's `approval-unlocked` r1 the element named "dark trousers", "hips and legs drawn in full" and "both boots planted flat on the flags with a soft dark painted shadow under each", and the ideogram returned a narrow pair of feet plus a **third leg and a third brown boot standing apart on the flagstones by the left edge**. Three shoes on one man, inside a box that held his whole body, so the geometry was not the cause. The prose carried a number ("both") but reached for it through the shadows, so the model had a boot and a shadow to draw twice and drew a third. Write the count as a fact about the picture, not only about the figure: "exactly two legs, one left and one right, grown from his hips; exactly two brown boots planted side by side, the only footwear anywhere on the flagstones; a single soft shadow under the pair and no other shadow on the floor". The check is a frame-wide count of shoes with their x-positions, then a 10x strip of each frame edge, because the extra one hides in the slab of floor beside him and a full-frame pass reports it as "extra legs" without saying where.

**Counting boots is not counting legs, and a third leg can end in mid-air.** The r2 re-render of that slot carried the sentence "exactly two legs, one left and one right, grown from his hips ... exactly two brown boots, the only footwear anywhere on the flagstones", and it was rejected on sight with "character has 3 legs". A third trouser leg hung between the two real ones and stopped above the floor with no boot on it, so the shoe count passed every crop while the leg count failed: the floor band held exactly two boots. A footless limb is invisible to a floor-level check, so the count has to be asked of the trousers on a crop of the belt-to-floor column: how many trouser legs leave the belt, and does each one end in a boot.

**When the count fails twice, remove the part — but the cut buys one attempt, not a cure.** The r3 fix for that slot stopped writing legs altogether: the man stands behind the bench, the bench's near edge crosses in front of him at the waist, and the element's `bbox` stops above that edge, so the picture holds head, neck, shoulders, arms, chest and belt and no lower body at all. Say it in both places, because either one alone loses: the prose names where the bench cuts him and that no leg, knee or boot is in the picture, and the box ends at the cut rather than running to the floor. The leg class does clear — the floor band came back as stone, wood and shadow only — and the failure moves up into the bust: that render had **no hands at all**, and one arm running as a smooth rounded limb behind and through the bench leg. A torso seen cropped at the waist is the one figure shape where the arms have no surface to end on, so the limb defects the hands rules exist for come back at the shoulders and elbows. Therefore the cut ships only after a whole-figure crop read that answers both questions — is each arm drawn from the shoulder, and where does each hand end — and a bust that fails either one is a hold-back, not a caveat. On the next miss the figure comes out of the frame entirely (the arithmetic above, applied to the body): a still life of the prop in its state carries the beat with nothing left to break, and it clears the limb class in one move where three prompt revisions had each traded one limb defect for another.

## The whole silhouette is its own read, and the pose decides it

A rejection can name the body rather than the hands ("body of main character is broken"), and a frame whose hands, props and palette are clean is still rejected whole on it. The self-check that reads fingers and lettering does not see this, because at frame size a stretched torso reads as a lean. So the body gets its own question, asked on a wide crop of the figure alone, before the hand crops.

Two poses break the silhouette, and both look harmless in the prompt:

- **the torso laid across a prop that runs the frame's width.** "Leaning his upper body over the horizontal pipe" draws the torso elongated along the pipe and effectively duplicated, rejoining a second set of legs at the far side — the picture has one head, one pair of shoulders and two lower bodies.
- **the figure folded low against a machine.** Cross-legged or crouched with the chest over the knees gives no waist: the torso runs into the lap and the hips turn away from the shoulders, so the upper and lower body face different directions.

Neither is fixed by re-describing the joints. Change what the picture contains: **an upright figure with the prop beside him, not under him.** A long horizontal object stays in the frame only as something he stands next to or looks at; state the posture as a static fact ("standing upright, his weight on both feet, his back straight, the tank beside him") rather than as an action ("leaning in to look"), because an action verb invites the pose that breaks the body. When the beat genuinely needs him low, cut him at the chest — a half-length figure cannot fail at the hips.

Ask the read for the four things that failed: head size against the torso, shoulder width against hip width, the length of the torso, and how the torso meets the hips. Add one yes/no that catches the duplication that photographs as a lean: **is any part of the body drawn twice?** A frame-level read describes the intent it was handed and will answer "leaning over the pipe" for a torso drawn twice; the wide figure crop plus that question is what turns it into a hold-back before a card spends his attention on it.

## Count the renders before writing the next prompt: a figure that keeps breaking is the concept, not the sentence

One slot can lose three renders to the same figure and each fails differently — the head fused into the chest, then a head over a box with a headless torso beside it, then a disembodied head alone on the desk — which is what makes it feel like progress while the defect is unchanged. The scene around the figure passed the owner's read every time, so the figure was the only defect and each re-description produced a new one.

**Count the renders before writing the next prompt, not after.** At two failed attempts on one figure, stop re-describing the body and change what the picture contains: take the figure out, reduce him to a hand and forearm entering the frame, or stand him behind something that legitimately hides the body. When the figure is the only thing the owner objected to, removing it clears the objection exactly, and the beat that survives is the one he said made sense — an object-only frame also cannot fail for anatomy reasons. `references/archive/re-render-defect-persistence.md` §7 carries the hand version of the same move.

**The three-render arithmetic in `references/archive/hold-back-or-ship.md` is a card's attention budget, not a cap on a slot nobody has seen.** Renders spent while a card is open cost his eye on that card; renders spent on a slot that has never reached a card cost only the queue, and a slot can legitimately run well past three there when each attempt clears a different clause. So count by defect, not by render: two misses of the same clause is the signal to change what the picture contains, and a slot whose successive attempts each fix one thing and drop another is progressing even at its fifth render. What must not happen is the opposite reading — a fifth render described the same way as the fourth, which is how one slot burns a night and still owes a card.

**The same count applies to a dressing prop, not only to a figure.** On that slot a potted plant on the shelf produced an impossible object in two consecutive renders — first its pot swallowed the handle of the hammer hanging beside it, then it came back as two pots with the second one hanging in mid-air under the shelf — and the fix that ended it was deleting the plant and letting the bare shelf plank hold that corner. A prop that cannot render cleanly twice is cheaper to remove than to describe a third time, and nothing in the beat depends on it. Say why in the card's note, because the post's author put the prop there.

**The composition belongs to the post's author, so a removal is a hand-off, not a unilateral call** — a child issue of the card assigned to the author, asking for the composition with no human figure (camera, crop, the props, the four corners, any text), then `blockedByIssueIds` on the card. List the failed attempts and what each drew, because the author needs to know the body is the thing that will not render rather than that the prompting was lazy. A figure-free answer comes back as a scene with joints of its own (a case with a plug, a machine with a flap), so check it for those instead of for limbs, and give every region a described prop — the space the figure vacated is the largest bare region in the frame and the first place the model pastes something.

**A removal that keeps the frame is not a composition change, so it needs no hand-off.** When the figure is the only thing that leaves — same camera, same room, same bench, same props in the same places — the author's composition is intact and the slot can be re-rendered in the run that found the defect: delete the figure element, re-aim the remaining `bbox`es at the props the beat rests on, and say in `high_level_description` that nobody is in the picture, with no hands, no arms, no legs and no figure of any kind, so the negative is not left to the elements. Write the beat into the prop's state instead (the lid lifted with a dark gap under it, the strap hanging off its empty staple, the lock standing open beside the tools) and check the frame for stray people on the full read before any crop. The hand-off is for a frame that needs a new camera, a crop or a new set of props — not for the same picture with the man taken out of it.

**A slot that owes two independent fixes takes the still life in one move.** When a slot's attempts have split across two defect families — a body clause that keeps missing and a prop drawn in its resting state instead of the state the section needs — do not spend a render deciding which to fix first: take the figure out and put both corrections into the props. The removal clears the body family outright, and a prop alone in a frame has no second subject to be ambiguous about, so the state clause has one thing to describe. Count the families, not the renders, when choosing this: two misses of one clause and one miss of another is the moment, not after a fourth attempt at the body.

## Keep the small prop plain, or it becomes another object

Every extra feature on a small metal prop invites a different reading. A brass cylinder described with a flange came back looking like a spent cartridge; the same cylinder written as "smooth and plain with flat ends, no flange, no ring, no cap and no bullet shape" read as a plain peg. Where the prop's *shape* is the story — a round peg against a long narrow slot — make the mismatch a stated proportion ("three times longer than the peg is wide and half as tall as it is thick") and set the prompt boxes so the proportion survives a crop.

## A prop shaped like a body part has to be checked as a prop, not as a hand

A pair of empty work gloves lying on a bench came back drawn correctly and was still the riskiest element in the frame: at full frame the read called them *glove-like hands*, which is both the pass and the failure written in one phrase. The beat was "the task was posted and nobody's hands were in it", so a frame whose gloves could be read as hands says the opposite of the section.

Settle it on a 3x crop of the prop alone, and ask what the object is, not whether it is a hand: the object needs its own features drawn — **visible cuffs, a hollow collapsed body, stitched seams, no fingernails, and no wrist or forearm running out of the shape into the frame edge**. Only that answers the question, because the two readings live about two pixels apart at blog scale.

The same check covers every body-shaped prop the series uses: a mitten, a boot standing on its own, a mask on a hook, a mannequin's hand, a jacket on a nail. Two consequences for the prompt: give the prop one object-identifying feature in its own `desc` ("palms up and empty, the leather stiff and creased, each with a wide cuff"), and never place a body-shaped prop where a real hand would be, because the model will finish the analogy and put an arm on it.

## An object that carries a label in real life renders with pseudo-text on it

The bar allows no lettering unless it is readable English, and the engines fill every **label surface** they recognise with letter-shaped marks that resolve to no word. `references/prompt-hygiene.md` carries the mechanism and the ban rules; what this file adds is the composition rule, because the object is the cause and the words are not.

A fire extinguisher on a wall bracket came back with a white label of blurred text lines, and it was the only defect in an otherwise clean frame. The prompt said "no labels" and it made no difference: an extinguisher, a gas bottle, a first-aid box, a shipping crate stencil and a warning plate all have a label as part of their identity, so plainness is not something an adjective can add.

- **Take the label-bearing object out of the composition.** Replace it with something that has no face to write on — a closed plain toolbox standing on the tiles, a bare shelf plank, a coiled hose — and say what replaced it in the same `desc`, so the element still fills its corner.
- If the object has to stay, give it a positive plainness clause on the object itself plus one room-level sentence ("every object in the room is plain: no labels, no plates, no writing on any of it"), and then buy at most one attempt with it.
- Check it on a crop of the object at 3x: a label reads as a light rectangle at frame scale and as pseudo-text only when cropped.

A tiny printed mark that is plausibly part of the object (a barcode on a cardboard box, a stamped plate on a machine) can be named as a caveat on the card instead of costing a render; a label carrying lines of text is not, because that is the invented-words family the campaign exists to remove.

## A bezel cluster is settled by the crop, never by the frame read

A screen whose beat needs only one flat shape still comes back with two or three window-control dots on the monitor's **bezel**, and a full-frame read reports that cluster on frames whose bezel is blank — so the claim goes to a 2x crop of that corner before it is written on a card or used to hold a render back, and the crop is also what clears it. The screen composition itself — one centred card, no title bar, no sidebar, no placeholder bars, and why those bars are pseudo-text even when `tesseract` finds no word — is in `references/archive/prompt-repair-audit.md` under *Translating a beat that lives on a screen*; do not write a second copy of that rule here.

## Choose a dressing prop that cannot carry text or a wrong size

The corner props are the ones nobody checks. The beat rides on one object, so every other prop reaches the card on a glance at the full frame — and a cellar frame whose oval track, crank and crate count were all correct was held for the spanner lying on the flags: several floor flags long, longer than the bench is wide, with faint stamped pseudo-lettering down its handle. Both faults showed at 4x and neither at frame scale, which is exactly the class that slips through.

- **A tool is the worst corner filler.** A spanner, a hammer, a saw, a gauge, a crate stencil: each has a handle or a face the engine wants to letter or mark, and each has a real-world size the model does not carry. Prefer something with no flat face and no size to miss — a short length of chain coiled flat, a folded cloth, a plain closed tin — and say what replaced what in the same `desc` so the corner stays filled.
- **Anchor the size to another object in the sentence**, not to an adjective: *a short length of chain no longer than the bench is wide*. `heavy` and `big` are read as licence, and an object on the floor gets scaled to furniture while a tool on a bench gets scaled to a tool.
- **Ban the marks at picture level, not object level.** A bare `no lettering` is read as covering signs and paper and leaves a metal handle free, so add the whole-frame sentence *no stamped, engraved or embossed mark on any metal surface in the picture*.
- **Crop the dressing props at 3x–4x before the card, not only the beat's object**, and ask what each corner object is and what size it reads as. A prop that is not the beat still holds the frame back, because he judges the whole picture.
- **When a repair fixes the beat and introduces a defect in a prop the repair added, the next render is subtractive** — drop the prop or swap it for one that cannot fail, keep the geometry that now works, and state the overage. That is not a third guess at the same clause.

## Check it on a crop, at two scales

A hand or a small mechanism is exactly the kind of detail a full-frame read smooths over: a full-frame pass called a merged hand "gripping the object", and the same frame at 3x read it as two thumbs and fused knuckles. Two independent reads that agree on the defect is enough to hold the render back; write the crop to disk with `scripts/crop-probe.py` and read the file, because the `region` parameter resolves to different content between calls. A read that says the pose is "natural" has settled nothing — count the arms, count the fingers, and check whether the object's parts still connect.

**Aim the crop before you trust it, because the element's `bbox` does not tell you where the hand ended up.** Three crops aimed by guesswork on one frame returned the waist, then the prop on its cradle, then the bench beside him, and each answered "no hand in this crop" — an answer that reads exactly like clearance and is the reason a bad render can survive a check. Locate first, then zoom: one wide pass over the whole figure at 3x–4x (or `frame-map.py` on the quadrant) to find where the arms actually end, and only then `--box` that spot at 6x.

**Find the figure by asking for its pixel bbox, not by hunting its colours.** A skin- or shirt-tone mask over the frame catches brick, bare wood, clay jars and — worst of all — the leather props that were the point of the picture, so it returns a bounding box spanning half the room and the crop built from it holds furniture and no figure. One call of the shape *give the approximate pixel bounding box of the standing man in this 1216x832 image as x1,y1,x2,y2* answers a real box, and the crop then wants a head-to-floor column roughly 340 px wide around it, upscaled 4x–5x, rather than a full-height strip at 300 px wide: a strip that thin blows up to a 1500x3700 image, the vision read downscales it, and the figure arrives too small to count anything on. A crop whose header region does not contain skin is a miss and must be re-aimed, never written into a note as "clean".

**A crop that cuts a limb off reports the limb as missing.** A 1.6x crop of the desk band was boxed to end at the man's second hand, and the read came back "only one hand is visible in this image — no second hand is present". That is the clause-1 defect (a missing hand) delivered by the crop's own frame, and taking it at face value would have held back a render that drew both fists correctly. Count limbs, heads and contacts only from a box that holds the whole figure's width — a single band across the figure at 1x–1.5x, scaled to about a thousand pixels wide, keeps the body and its context together and is the cheapest trustworthy count. **A crop holding one hip, waistband or pocket invents a hand that is not there**: a fold in the trousers beside the far fist reads as a third hand once the arm it belongs to is outside the box, and three crop reads on one frame produced a phantom hand the whole-figure view disproved. So a third-hand or third-arm claim needs the whole-figure crop before it costs a render, and the per-hand crops answer only the digit question afterwards. Then take each hand separately, tight, when the question is digits, because the two crops answer different questions and only the tight one over-reads a segment.

**A soft refusal is not a rate limit and not a finding.** A reader answers `I'm having a hard time fulfilling your request` — or one line of deflection — when the crop is asked as a closed question (`is the blade stopped outside the face?`, `is this hand fused?`), and the same pixels answer at once when the request is a plain description: `describe what this crop shows, part by part: the key, the opening behind it, and how they sit`. Re-ask that way once before concluding anything. A refusal copied into a check note reads as evidence about the picture when it is only evidence about the question, and the same holds for a `Sorry, something went wrong` on a wide crop: retry once as description and keep the file on disk so the second read sees the exact same pixels.

The description also settles the case two closed reads answered oppositely. One key's two reads came back `inserted into the vertical keyhole` and `entirely outside it, running parallel to the hole` — the part-by-part description named the bow, the shank and the bit overlapping the slot's lower half, which is the fact the beat rested on (`this key does not fit this door`) and neither yes/no had given. So treat a leading question's answer as one reader's guess, and settle a fit, a contact or a count on the description of the parts rather than on a verdict-shaped question.

**But when the two reads disagree about whether anything is drawn in a region at all, neither read is the tool** — a blank band against painted scenery is a pixel count, not a judgement. `scripts/check-pale-band.py <png> --columns <x0>,<x1>` prints the dark-pixel share per sampled column: ink and hatching leave 9–15% with a wide spread, an unrendered fill leaves near zero with a flat one. Run that script rather than writing the same profile inline in a heredoc — an ad-hoc version picks its own dark threshold and prints no per-column list, so its numbers cannot be compared with the ones already in the notes. `references/archive/hold-back-or-ship.md` carries the case where one crop read invented a panel border that was not in the file.

**An open palm with spread fingers is the second hand shape that fails on both engines**

Beside the grip, the raised open palm is the other pose that never survives. At blog-header scale a spread hand is a fan of five thin shapes, and both engines draw it as a convoluted mass with no countable digits — on post 043 the same gesture failed the ideogram for the dead-endpoint slot twice, once as a waving palm and once as a palm held above a tray. Flux is no better; the post-041 header cost five flux attempts on a raised hand.

The lesson generalises: an open hand of any kind is unreliable. Post 043 tested the mild version directly — the prompt said "both arms hang straight down and both hands are open and empty at his sides, no fingers spread wide, nothing held in either hand" — and both engines drew palms turned outward with too many fused digits, on both hands, on two different slots. An open hand is a fan of thin shapes whether it is spread wide or hanging loose, and "no fingers spread wide" did not save it.

**The one reliable answer is no hand drawn at all — but hide the arms behind a described SURFACE, never behind the body.** Arms that run down behind a bench top, a desk edge or the far side of a crate leave the frame's own object exactly where the forearm would end, so there is nothing to draw; that is what saved the waiting-slot render and every fist-on-desk frame since. The same sentence written as *both arms run down behind his back* puts the end of the arm inside the silhouette, so the model has to finish the limb and closes it as a **rounded stump with no forearm, no wrist and no hand** — clause 1, and it reads worse than the fused hand it replaced. Settle it on a 4x–5x crop of the shoulder; a whole-figure read calls the stump "an arm tucked behind him" and passes it. When no surface can hide the arms, take the figure out of the frame: a body has no way to end an arm invisibly. Second best is a closed fist resting on a surface, stated as "one simple rounded fist, no separate fingers", counted on a crop at 5x-6x. Anything else costs two renders and a night of queue time.

**A fist held up in the air is not the safe option — it comes back as a duplicated arm.** A chest-height fist with nothing under it failed both engines on the same prompt: ideogram and flux each drew the **whole forearm twice**, a second arm running down from the same shoulder with a stump or a third hand at the elbow, so the frame has three arms and three hands. The words `exactly two arms and two hands` were already in the `high_level_description` and did not outvote the pose. What distinguishes this from a fused hand is that it is not a small detail: at 2x on the figure it is obvious, and it is clause 1, so it is held back.

The cause is the pose, and the same cause produced the raised open palm and the raised-hand grip before it: a limb with its end free in the air has no surface to be anchored to, so the model carries the shape on down and closes it a second time. **Give the fists a surface to rest on and describe that surface as its own element with its own `bbox`** — a plain work stool on the floor, "four straight legs, a square worn seat, the man's two fists resting flat on the seat" — so the hands have a named place to land instead of open air. That is the pose that passed on the crates, the desk edge and the stool seat; a raised fist has passed nowhere.

**A surface anchors the arm; it does not guarantee the hand, and the two engines fail the same surface pose differently.** One prompt written exactly that way — both arms hanging straight down, each hand closed into one simple rounded fist resting flat on a stool seat — came back from flux as two clean closed fists with a thumb each, and from ideogram with both hands pressed onto the bench edge drawn as blocks of vertical ridges and no countable digits at 4x. The words were identical; only the engine differed. So write the contact as flat and closed rather than as a weight on an edge ("both hands resting flat on top of the seat, the fingers of each hand folded under, no fingers curled over the edge"), and settle it per engine: read each file on its own, because a clean companion says nothing about the main render. When one engine still fuses where the other passed, that is a **single-engine slot** — ship the passing render, name the held job id and its defect on the card's face, and do not spend a third render restoring symmetry; the brief carries the next pose (arms hidden behind a bench top or desk edge, so no hand is drawn anywhere) in case he rejects it.

Consequence for the check: on a figure whose arms are meant to be down, the crop question is **count the arms, not the fingers**. One wide 2x crop of the figure is enough to see a duplicated forearm, and it has to be asked before the hand-level crops, because a duplicated arm survives every finger question — each of its hands is individually fine.

**A hand curled round a handle fails on both engines — give the prop a rest instead**

A grip is the smallest curled shape in the frame: five digits closed round a cylinder a few dozen pixels wide. Flux fuses them into one mass; ideogram keeps a thumb on top and three merged knuckles underneath, which reads plausible at full frame. Cropping at 4x settles it either way, and cropping is the only way to settle it — a full-frame read called the ideogram's grip "fingers clearly separated" and the crop disagreed.

Do not reword the grip and do not try the other engine: both fail on the same prompt shape. Rewrite the composition so nobody holds the prop. A magnifier stands upright in a plain wooden rest with its lens turned to what it enlarges; a lamp sits on its own base; a lever hangs from its pivot. The figure then gets hands that draw nothing to go wrong — one simple rounded fist each on a desk edge, or the arms taken down behind the figure so no hand is drawn at all. Never fall back to "open and empty at his sides": that reads as an open palm and both engines put too many digits on it.

**State the floor, or the figure floats**

A correct painted floor does not put the man on it. The ideogram header of post 042 came back with the flagged floor drawn properly edge to edge and the man and the boiler both hovering two brick courses above it, no contact and no shadow: at full frame it reads as two cut-outs pasted on a background, which fails the bar's "one continuous scene" clause even though every limb, prop and corner is correct. The prompt had said "standing on the floor" only in passing and never asked for a shadow.

Fix the wording in both places, not just one: in `background`, name the floor as running unbroken to the foot of the wall and say every object in the room stands on it; in the figure's `desc` and in each floor-standing prop's `desc`, say the shoes are planted flat and the legs rest on the stone, each with a soft dark painted shadow underneath. A shadow is what proves contact — without one the model has nothing to attach the object to. Check it before the hands: a floating figure survives a full-frame read that is looking only at fingers.

**Check the grounding on a tight crop, not a wide strip.** The re-render of that header came back grounded — soles on the tiles, shadow beneath — and it still got called floating twice, once by a full-frame read and once by a 2x crop of the bottom band of the picture. The cause was the crop, not the render: a wide strip of cel-shaded stone with a hard shadow edge reads as air under the feet, and a crop aimed by the prompt's `bbox` lands wherever the figure did not. Locate the figure in **pixels** first (ask for its pixel bbox, or scale the prompt box by the image size), then cut a small region around the contact point at 4x-5x. A verdict of "floating" is only trustworthy from a crop that shows the sole and the floor line in the same small frame.

**The same tight crop governs a PROP whose beat is its distance from the floor.** "Lifted clear of the flags" and "still resting on them" are the whole before/after of many slots, and an object's contact point is a few pixels tall at frame scale, so a crop of the floor around it answers nothing. Ask it of the contact edge itself at 5x-6x: is there a lit strip of floor between the object's bottom plank and its shadow, and does the shadow touch the object. A 3x crop reads the shadow's dark edge as contact and reports "resting on the tiles"; at 5x and again at 6x the same pixels show a strip about a quarter of the plank's height with a separate shadow, which is a lifted object. **A marginal answer is a caveat, not a hold:** when the gap reads at 5x and the shadow is separate, the frame ships with `the air gap under it is narrow, about a quarter of a plank` written on the item, because the pair still reads and a render spent on four inches of shadow costs a night of queue time. Hold only when the object plainly touches what the beat says it has left.

## A repeated cast multiplies the body risk, and an airborne verb is what lifts it

A frame whose point is several identical copies of one helper — a queue of the same android at a board, a line of them at a bench — carries the count sentence for the *set* and still comes back with each copy broken in its own way: two of three hovering above the floor, one with a single leg, one with an arm fused into the back of its head. The count in `high_level_description` says how many there are and nothing about the body of any one of them, so the model distributes the missing parts across the copies and every copy passes a frame-level read because each one carries only one fault.

Write the body into **every** copy's own element, not once for the set:

- "exactly two legs, both drawn whole from hip to floor, both feet planted flat on the floorboards", plus "exactly two arms: the right arm raised holding X, the left arm hanging down at its side", plus "nothing hangs behind its head" when a raised arm shares the space above the shoulders.
- Replace every pose verb that has no surface under it. "Caught mid-step", "slightly tilted forward", "leaning in to look" all resolve to a jump or a hover in a frame where several figures share one floor, and the copy that got the verb is the one that leaves the floor. A static state does the same job: "standing one step behind it, both feet flat on the floorboards".
- Keep the ground clause in `background` as for a single figure — the floor runs unbroken to the wall and every object in the room stands on it — so the copies have one surface to be planted on rather than a painted backdrop behind them.

The check is a count of copies and legs on one crop wide enough to hold two adjacent figures: a floating copy reads as a jump at frame scale and as a body with no feet once cropped, and an arm ending behind a head reads as a shoulder seam until the crop has the head and both arms in it. **Check each engine's file on its own**, because the same prompt can come back whole on one and broken on the other — a companion that stands every copy cleanly says nothing about the ideogram, and the ideogram is what decides whether the slot ships.

## One small figure comes back as two — check the frame edges for a twin

A short full-body character drawn in a symmetrical room was duplicated: one complete figure in front of the rack and a second, identical one cut by the left edge, mirrored in pose, clothing and boots. The prompt said "one figure, exactly two arms and two hands" and the count was satisfied twice over, so the count clause did not stop it — the duplicate is the model balancing a symmetric composition, and it is clause 1 whether or not the second copy is complete.

- Write the singleton twice, in the `high_level_description` **and** in the character element: "exactly one person in the whole picture, nobody else anywhere in the room or at any edge, and no reflection or mirrored copy of him". The room-edge wording is what matters: a duplicate usually appears cropped at a side edge, where a whole-frame read reports it as "a partial figure at the border" and a count of "people" answers one.
- **A partial figure at the edge is still a second person.** Do not accept "it mirrors him, so it reads as a reflection" from a read: at 2x the second body has its own boots, hair and raised fist. Crop the suspect edge and count bodies, not faces.
- The same failure arrives from a symmetric backdrop — an electrical panel on one side invites a counterpart on the other. Break the symmetry with a deliberately uneven prop set instead of two balanced corners.
- **The doubled clause buys one attempt, not immunity: on the second duplication the figure comes out of the frame.** A rack centred in a symmetrical room reproduced the twin even with the singleton written twice — in `high_level_description` and on the character element, edge wording included — and this time the second copy was a **whole second chibi** standing on the other side of the rack, not a fragment cut by an edge. So the edge-crop check above finds nothing, and a frame-level count of people is the check that sees it. Two misses of the same clause means changing what the picture contains rather than rewording it a third time: the beat then rides on the machine's own state (every bay lit, the room otherwise empty) with no figure left to duplicate. Ask the people count on the full frame before cropping edges whenever the composition is symmetrical — a symmetry that balances a centred object will balance it with a second body.
- **A duplicated figure does not always stand beside him — the engine sometimes welds the spare body to him or floats it next to him, so a "nobody else in the room" clause reads as satisfied.** Two frames on one batch, both against a bare brick wall with the man in a tall narrow box: one drew a **body-shaped sack with stitched eyes and a mouth** slipped onto his back, and the other drew a **human head and shoulders on a long smooth tube** hovering beside him, casting its own shadow. Neither is a second person at an edge, so the edge crop and the people count both pass; the check that sees them is the whole-figure crop read for *anything else with a head or a face*. The fix is structural, not a stronger singleton: **narrow the figure's box** (that batch went from y 300-950 x 90-320, i.e. 230×650, down from 320×750) and ban the objects by name in the `high_level_description` — *nothing carried on his back and nothing strapped to him: no bag, no sack, no pack, no dummy, no mannequin, no stuffed figure, and nothing anywhere in the room with a face or eyes drawn on it; nothing floats or stands beside him, no second head, no figure-shaped object*.

**The spare limb also arrives as a SLEEVE in the arm-and-hip overlap, where a wide read calls it a fold.** A flux frame of a man with both arms hanging straight down came back with an extra sleeve-and-arm contour hanging behind his near arm, in the shadow where the arm meets the body: garment-toned and contour-shaped, so it is invisible to a skin-tone or a hand-digit check and it is not a second person, so the people count passes too. It is clause 1 all the same. A 2x figure crop called it a fold and 6x of the hip disagreed, so settle this class the way the crop rules already say — the 2x figure to locate, then a 6x of the arm against the torso, then one 4x of the whole torso — and hold only when the reads at different offsets agree. In the prompt, say each arm is drawn once from its shoulder and that nothing hangs behind him or between his arm and his body; where the arms stay down, keeping him a clear hand's width off the furniture and out of deep shadow removes the space the contour gets drawn into.

## A contact clause on the helper is read as a limb on the man

A flux2-klein render of the post-049 `ip-flux` beat grew a third limb: a grey segmented mechanical arm out of the protagonist's own shoulder, ending in a mechanical hand on the conveyor. The prompt had the grey android helper "lean its elbow on the far side of the belt" - a contact clause belonging to a secondary character, which the engine attached to the nearest human instead. Settle it on two crops (1.5x of the figure, 3x of the torso: the limb's top end sits under his sleeve, not on the android) and fix it by removing the contact, not by adding another "exactly two arms": the android now stands a step back with both stubby arms at its sides. The same frame also showed the engine difference the pair exists for - a read called it a modern digital cartoon with a grain overlay rather than a cel - so a flux companion can fail the style clause and the anatomy clause in one render; name both in the held-back record.

## A companion animal's traits migrate onto the person

When the frame holds a small animal mascot beside the figure — the series fox, a cat, a bird — the model blends the two bodies instead of drawing two subjects: the person comes back with the animal's tail curling out of his waist, and the animal comes back with a third ear or the wrong number of legs. It recurred across both engines on one slot, with the animal in its own element and its own `bbox`, so proximity is the cause and no rewording of either `desc` fixes it. Two misses of this class on one slot means the animal leaves the picture: drop its element, re-aim the remaining boxes at the props the beat rests on, and put the negative in `high_level_description` — `no animals in the picture, no fox, no tail on the person, no animal ears on his head` — because the absence has to be stated at picture level, not left to the missing element. The beat keeps the figure and loses the mascot, which is the trade the anatomy bar makes. A single miss is settled on two crops: 4x of the figure's waist, where a tail reads as a fold of the shirt, and 4x of the animal's head, where an extra ear reads as the ear's own crease.

## When it fails, re-render rather than argue

The fix for a mangled hand or a hybrid mechanism is a prompt rewrite, never a note on the card: he reads a broken limb or a nonsense object as the picture being unusable. Say in the held-back attempt's record that it was held back at the self-check and never shown to him, and keep the attempt suffix on the render name so the slot's history shows both.

## A sixth-digit claim needs the tightest crop before it costs a render

A 6x crop of a fist that also kept the forearm in frame read "a thumb on top and five finger segments underneath, so six digits"; an 8x crop of the fist alone, arm out of frame, read an ordinary five-digit clenched fist with no sixth finger. The loose crop adds the arm's own contour as a segment, so a mid-zoom read over-counts where a wide read under-counts. Take the digit count from the tightest crop in which the whole hand fills the frame, and treat any extra-digit or extra-limb claim as unsettled until that crop exists — the same rule that stops a loose crop costing a render on a hand that carries five also stops it costing one on a hand that carries five and a half segments of shading.

**The same rule governs a companion you are about to hold back.** A flux companion whose book-holding hand read "fused" at 3x and "six digits" at 5x is still unsettled when both crops kept the wrist in frame, and the compliant move is one tight crop of the hand alone before the hold-back is written. The alternative is to name the count as a residual on the card and let the slot ship on its ideogram; holding a companion back on a loose crop spends the owner's attention on a defect that may not exist.

**Two hand crops that overlap count one hand twice.** A bench-area box and a machine-area box aimed at the two contacts of one figure each contained the same open hand resting on the bench, and the two reads added to a phantom third hand on a frame that carried two. Treat the counts as a union, not a sum: aim boxes that share no region, or write both boxes' coordinates in the note so the overlap is visible instead of being re-derived as a defect.

## A figure behind furniture and his shoes in the bottom of the frame cannot both be drawn

One sentence pair is geometrically self-contradictory and the engine resolves it by cutting the man in half: `stands behind the bench` plus `shoes flat on the concrete inside the bottom of the frame`. If he stands behind the bench, its apron hides his legs; the clause that puts his boots in the bottom of the frame puts them in front of it. On post 073's flux header the render came back with his torso ending at the bench top and his boots planted in front of the bench, no hips between — a body fused into the furniture, which is a figures-correct hold-back and not a caveat to name.

- **Write the figure clear of the furniture from the first pass.** Stand him beyond the end of the bench or table, on open floor, and keep three clauses that make the separation explicit: `clear floor all around his whole body`, `nothing crossing his waist, hips or legs`, `his trousers running unbroken from waistband to shoes`. Drop `behind the bench` entirely rather than softening it.
- **Judge it on a written 2x whole-figure crop, and let two readers decide.** The full-frame read of the same flux frame reported two fists and no anatomy error; the 2x nearest crop and a 5x waist crop both reported the fusion, and a second reader on the 2x crop answered the one-line question `is his body cut in half by the bench, or normal?` with `cut in half`. Keep the question to one line on that script, because a three-part question comes back empty with `finish_reason=length`.
- **The two engines do not share the defect rate here, so judge each frame on its own crop.** The same contradictory sentence on the ideogram read as normal occlusion behind the bench on both readers, and its header stayed on the card while the flux twin came off. A failing companion is evidence about the companion, never about the slot.
- **Compare the figure's `bbox` against every waist-height object's box before you submit.** A desk, bench, counter, crate or stool whose box overlaps the figure's box between waist and knee puts the furniture in front of the body by geometry, and the engine then ends the torso on that object's top edge — the same fused-at-the-waist defect the contradictory sentence pair produces, arriving from the boxes while every sentence is correct. `check-bboxes.py` reports each element against its own `desc` and prints "no bbox contradicts its own desc", because the overlap between two elements is not a contradiction it tests. Read the pairs yourself, and fix it in the composition rather than the prose: move the object clear of his column, or drop it when it is decoration. In a frame where his contact with that object is not the beat, an object out of his box costs nothing and a fused waist costs a render.

## A post's slots are one sequence, so check the states against each other

A three-slot post whose sections tell one story reads best when the three frames share one room and one repeated object, and that object's **state** carries the arc: a brass bell clapper-out with motion arcs in the header, the same bell hanging still with its clapper at rest on the resolution; a switchboard with three cords pulled out and lying slack on the flags, then the same board with every socket seated. The trio then reads as a sequence, and each frame gets a proof an adjective cannot give.

Two consequences for the check. **Verify the flip by comparing the two frames to each other, not each against its own prompt** — a "still bell" that is only a quieter ringing bell passes every single-frame read, and the two crops side by side are what exposes it. And **write the state each slot must show into its item description**, because the second frame of a pair is where the engine re-draws the first one out of habit. Submit the set as one batch so the pair is judged together and the comparison exists before any of it reaches a card.

## A beat that means "there is no room" must not be drawn as a container with compartments

An empty slot is the strongest signal in the frame, and it says the opposite of a full one. Two consecutive attempts at one slot — a rack of fifteen pigeonholes with only fourteen crates and the spare standing on the floor — both left one compartment empty, and a reader takes that as *the crate came out of there*: room existed, so nothing was full. No wording fixed it; `every slot filled, not one empty slot anywhere` was in the prompt on the second attempt and the engine emptied the bottom row anyway, because a grid of slots with one crate outside it is its own story.

The fix is an object with **no compartments to leave empty**: a chest packed so full that its lid rides up on the contents and the clasp no longer meets its hasp, with the spare crate standing outside it. Nothing in that frame can be read as a space the spare would fit into. When a beat is a *quantity that does not fit*, choose a vessel whose fill is shown by the lid that will not shut or the door that will not close, never by a grid with a gap in it.

The same shape applies to a **seated** state: a bolt whose head stands proud of its bracket reads as slid out and loose, whatever the prose says, because every training picture of a bolt has a head away from the plate. State the head flush against the plate with no bare shaft visible between them, and the spring behind it crushed to a stack of coils touching with no daylight between any two turns. Both were needed before that slot read as *home*.

## A count across many objects is drawn against one shared reference

When a section says *the same correction was made to N things* — nine configs, six services, three pods — do not ask for N objects each in the corrected state and trust the reader to notice the sameness. N separate objects are N chances to drift, and a reader handed nine of anything reads the arrangement rather than the correction.

Carry the count and the sameness on **one shared reference every object has to meet**:

- nine identical pale planks lying in one evenly spaced row on the joiner's bench, every near end cut to meet **one taut cord strung between two small iron pins**, none overhanging it and none falling short;
- the pieces the correction removed **kept in the frame as evidence** — the nine trimmed slivers, all cut to the same length, heaped in a tin tray at the near corner — so the number is visible twice, once in the row and once in the offcuts.

State the count in the picture's own terms (`nine identical planks`, `all of them the same width and thickness`) and say that no object overhangs or falls short of the reference: a model that wants variety staggers them along the line, and the sameness dies without anything looking broken. Settle it on a crop that holds the reference and several ends at once, because a row of ends reads as tidy at frame scale however they sit.

**At N=2 the count is the whole beat, and "side by side" collapses into one object.** Two identical parts whose *states* differ — one eaten through and one sound, one shut and one open — is the commonest contrast in the series and the one a count clause does not survive: a walkway asked as `a walkway of exactly two oak planks laid side by side, each end of both resting square on a trestle`, with the near plank broken and the far one whole, came back as **one wide board broken at mid-span**. Side by side plus near/far is a contradiction — a reader cannot tell which axis the two planks are separated on — so the engine drew the one board both halves were describing, and the frame then said "a rotten walkway" rather than "one of the two carried everything". Write the pair as geometry a draughtsman would place, and say the count three times: a clear gap of a hand's width between them; each named by its own position, not by near and far but by *the plank nearest the viewer* and *the identical plank directly behind it*; each with its own state; then "there are exactly two planks in the picture and no third plank, no board and no beam of any kind". Check the count on a wide crop of the span, because two planks seen end-on read as one thick plank.

**A set of faults reads as a row of objects each mended in its own way.** When the section is *three things went wrong and each had its own fix*, put three objects along one bench, each plainly mended, and let the repairing differ: a hatch whose hasp is closed through a padlock while the key that fits it hangs on the neighbouring pier out of reach; a kettle with a wide bright scorch ring burned into the boards round its base and its lid propped open on a bent nail; a bucket under a brass tap whose handle is tied off with wire, already half full. The room and the camera stay the same across the three; the difference is the repair, which is what the section is about.

Both shapes are still lifes, which is why they are the cheap slots: nobody in the frame means no limb to lose, and the reference line or the mended state is a geometric fact the engine can only draw right or wrong.

## A beat that is a choice is drawn by where the figure stands

A comparison — the small thing against the big thing, the tool he took against the one he refused — invites two shapes that both fail. A **pointing hand** is an open hand, and an open hand of any kind comes back as a fan with too many fused digits. A **left/right split of the frame** reads as two pictures pasted together: each half gets its own light and its own finish, so the frame stops being one continuous scene.

Draw the decision as a place rather than a gesture:

- give the refused option every mark of complication — a tall machine of exposed gears, four belts running out to four sub-assemblies, panels standing open on their hinges, loose tools dropped on the floor beneath it;
- give the chosen option nothing but output — one small plain machine on a swept-clean bench, a single hopper of stock at its top, a neat stack of finished pieces in its tray;
- stand the single figure at the chosen object with **his back square to the refused one**, both fists resting flat on the bench's near edge, one on either side of the finished pieces, head down at them. **Do not put his hands in his pockets for this beat**, even though it is the pose the sentence above used to recommend: the waist is the one region where every zoom level invents a limb, so a pockets pose can be neither passed nor condemned by reading it — see the phantom-limb rule in the crop section, and take a bench edge, a crate rim or a desk edge as the surface for the fists instead.

The choice then reads from where he is standing and what he has already made, with nothing pointed at, no arrow and no split frame. Say the hands' state in the same sentence as the stance, because a figure turned away from the camera is where a spare sleeve or a third hand appears.

## A thing that holds nothing is an open frame you see through

When the section's point is that something keeps no state — a stateless service with no database behind it, a machine with no tank — do not draw an empty space where the store would be. An absence is the one thing this engine will not draw, and a described emptiness beside an object invites a container to arrive and fill it.

Put the state into the object's own construction: build it as an **open frame** — one square beam across the top, one across the bottom, four uprights joining them, and the painted wall visible straight through the middle of the machine — and say in its own element that it holds nothing (no tank, no reservoir, no hopper, no drawer). The see-through middle is positive form: the reader is looking *through* the machine rather than at an empty place beside it, so there is nothing for the engine to fill.

To make the contrast explicit, put one older object that does hold something at the frame's edge and say in one sentence that it is the only object in the picture that does. Then attach whatever the section says is watching it: four small bells on one iron rail above, each wired down to a lug on the top beam, carries "the same four alerts, and nothing here to keep" without a single mark.

**The same positive-form rule covers a thing that has been shut: draw the closure as an added object, never as fittings taken off.** A door whose beat is *every way in has been removed* — handle, keyhole and knocker all gone, only plugged holes and a pale patch of unstained wood left — comes back with the fittings still on it: a handle, two keyholes, and the pale patch drawn as a small pale panel that reads as a placard. Removal is an absence and an absence does not draw; the pale patch is also the one mark that recruits a label. So express the closure by what was **added**: four stout oak boards nailed flat across the door's outside, each running its full width, a pair of square iron nails at each end with the heads proud, fresh saw cuts on the board ends, and the front way shut for good — beside it a narrow side gate standing ajar with a trodden track running to it. Boards nailed across a door are an object in an unambiguous pose; a door with its handle missing is a door the model will give a handle back. Keep the old fittings in the negative clause only ("no handle, no keyhole, no latch, no knocker, no plate, no pale patch") and let the positive form carry the state.

## A brand name in the prompt becomes readable text on a surface

When a prompt names a real product or brand ("a dark grey Framework laptop", "a silver Mac laptop"), the model can render that brand name as readable text on the casing or screen, even when the prompt carries "no text, no labels, no signs." The word came from the vocabulary, not the composition: the model reads the brand name as an instruction to write it on the surface. This is distinct from the lone-prop-on-empty-floor case (engine-pitfalls #42), where the model invents a word from the composition; here the model copies a word it was given.

Fix by removing the brand name and describing the object generically ("a dark grey laptop", "a silver laptop"), then strengthening the negative to name the specific thing that appeared: "no brand names, no logos, no lettering of any kind on any surface, including laptop screens and casings." The generic description plus the named negative is what clears it; the brand name alone, even with "no text," does not.


## The negative cast statement has to name every shape you are forbidding

"One person only" in the high-level description is a positive count, and this engine reads a count as a target rather than a ceiling: in a scene with two stations (a print at one end of a bench, a canvas at the other) it puts a figure at each station and the frame ends up with two operators. The flux companion of the same scene avoided the duplicate because its prose carried the negative cast statement explicitly — "one person only in the picture, no second person, no crowd" — while the ideogram, which said only "one person only, exactly two arms and two hands," came back with two figures and had to be silently re-rendered.

So state the cast negatively and exhaustively in `high_level_description`: "one person only in the whole picture, no second person, no crowd, no helper, no assistant." Then, in every element `desc` that names a station or object, state that it stands alone — "the finished canvas stands on its easel by itself; nobody stands beside it" — because two stations read as an invitation for two operators even when the cast line is correct. The negative cast statement is what the engine obeys; the positive count is what you count afterwards.

Settle a duplicate-figure claim by splitting the frame, not by asking the whole frame how many people are in it: write the left and right halves to disk at 2x and ask each half one scoped question — is there a human figure in this crop, and what is it doing. One figure in each half means two in the frame, and that is a show-bar failure on clause 1 (figures correct), not a quirk to note on the card.
