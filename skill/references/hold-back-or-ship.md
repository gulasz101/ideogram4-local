# Hold the render back, or ship it with the residual named?

The self-check's only job is to keep defects off a frozen card. It never approves anything. This file is the decision it has to make on every render that is not clean, and the render-count arithmetic that sits beside it.

The rule of thumb: **hold back what makes the picture say something its section does not; name everything else on the card and let the owner rule.** Both halves are load-bearing — a self-check that ships everything pastes defects in front of him, and one that holds everything costs 21 minutes a time for a mark he would have accepted.

## Hold back

- **A copied subject.** The picture's whole point is one person doing one thing and the render draws two identical people side by side. Decide it by which figure the beat depends on: an extra body in the background is a note, a copy of the protagonist is a hold-back. A card that carries the duplicate is a card he rejects for a defect you already read.
- **A held-up prop that no hand touches, when the action is holding it.** This one used to sit under *ship* and one verdict moved it: a rotation render put the fresh key a centimetre above a closed fist with no contact, the card named the gap in the item description as a caveat, and his rejection reason was exactly that gap ("main character is not actually holding the key in left hand, key is kind of drawn above his hand"). The beat read fine on the page and he still called it broken, so the render cost a round and the slot got nothing from it. Decide it on the contact point at 4x or more, never on a full-frame read. **The contact that counts is the shaft or handle inside the closed fingers, not the whole prop:** a key whose bow stands proud of the knuckles while its shaft runs through the fist is held, not floating, so do not read the bow's clearance as the gap and hold back a correct render for it. The repair is in the prompt: describe the fist and the key as one object (the bow standing out of the top of the closed fist, the knuckles pressed to the metal), which is what the corrected attempt did.
- **A connector that does not reach its target**, a prop the model invented in place of a described one, unreadable or misspelt text the prompt specified exactly, a missing story element.
- **A defect class the library has never approved.** Count it rather than argue about it: `scripts/verdict-class-rate.py --class <class>` prints rejected-vs-approved. A class with several rejections and no approval is a class the owner rejects, and a re-render costs 21 minutes while the same defect left on a card costs a whole round of his attention *plus* a render. Read the number with its limit — `0 approved` means no approval row proves he accepted it, not that he never did. **The count establishes that the class exists, not that it applies to the element in front of you, so read the subject of each rejection row before you hold anything back.** A class earns its rejections on renders where the trait carried the beat or broke the action — an arrow that turned the story around, a count the section's argument rested on. The same trait sitting *beside* the beat is a different finding: a decorative arrow in a corner, a grid pattern on a surface nothing hinges on. A batch of four renders once carried exactly those residuals — an even grid of identical windows, arrows pointing into the openings rather than out, a prop of the wrong proportion, tags reading as plain rounded rectangles — and was shipped with each one named on the card in the round after a shared "makes no sense" rejection; all four were approved. What carried them was each section's beat reading. So the hold-back question is always "does this trait make the picture say something its section does not", never "is this trait in a class that has rejections" — and naming it in his terms is the difference between a defect he approves knowingly and one he finds himself.

## Ship with the residual named

- **A NEW small artifact that does not break the beat**, especially once the slot has already spent several renders inside one round. Stray strokes lying flat across a prop off its own perspective, an extra background body, a duplicated gauge, a prop count one or two off the prompt, a gaze one shade weaker than asked, a stamped part number or a single numeral set on a fitting where such a mark belongs. Name it in the item description in his terms and let him weigh it.

  **The boundary is whether the surface could carry that mark in the world, and a plain object the prompt declared blank cannot.** A part number stamped into a casting, a pressure figure on a gauge body or a stencil on a crate is how that object is marked; a numeral on a plain wooden box in a corner, in a frame whose prompt said every surface is bare and nothing is written anywhere, is the model inventing lettering, and it holds that render under clause 3 however small and faded it is. Settle it with one crop of the object (`crop-probe.py <png> --box x0,y0,x1,y1 --scale 4`); a reader that names a readable `10` on a blank box is reporting the defect, and a mark it can only call `dashes` is the illustration convention that ships with a clause. **A plate bolted to a fitting is where a part or calibration numeral lives, so a digit there is that mark's home and ships with the clause named.** A small rectangular plate on a pipe collar, a casting number, a pressure figure on a gauge body — write it in his terms (`the collar carries a small plate with a digit on it`) and let him weigh it. Do not rest a clause-3 hold on that digit alone. The render that drew one also came back with a width relation too flat for its section (a mouth one and a half to two times a collar that needed four), and the relation is what justified the re-render; the numeral became the headline afterwards, which is how a shipping residual gets mis-remembered as a holding one and why a later run may hold a clean frame for a plate that belonged there.

**When the twin engine of the same slot is clean, the slot ships on the twin and the marked render waits in staging** — the hold costs no render, and swapping a passing frame in behind a defect the other engine never had is the cheapest repair in the book.
- **A state of many identical parts that came out partly lit, opened or tagged is a caveat, not a hold-back.** A prompt that asks for a lamp on each of four bays, a tag on every crate, a latch on both doors, and a render that lights two bays or tags three crates has drawn no malformed thing — the count is short, and a short count is not a defect class he rejects whole frames over. Ship it with the count in the item description in his terms (`the rack reads as up, not as every bay up`) when the section's beat survives the shortfall, and hold it back only when the section's argument **is** the count: a paragraph whose point is that all four came back wants four lit. **Count it on a crop of the object, never on the full frame** — unlit rows and unopened hatches read as shadow at card size, so the full-frame read reports a state that is not there.
- **A single object's state that comes back different ships with the clause, as long as it does not change what the picture asserts.** The test is the section's own sentence, not the prompt line. A padlock lying shut on the bench beside a chest whose strap is already off and whose lid is lifted still says the tools are reachable, because nothing is being held. A catch-plate bolted slightly below the latch pivot instead of exactly level still says the latch misses it. A notched quadrant that comes back with six notches and the pin in the first instead of five and the second still says the marker moved one step and the machine followed. A chisel and two mallets where the prompt asked for a chisel and one still says a few tools were left out. Ship each one with the deviation in his terms (`its padlock on the bench reads shut, but the strap is off and the lid is up`), so a yes is an informed yes. The same state is a hold-back the moment it flips the conclusion — a latch that engages says the lock works, seven padlocks say the count is seven — so the question is what the frame now asserts, never how far it sits from the prompt.
- **The residual that survives a fix he asked for.** A defect that shrinks across attempts is progress, not a failed fix, so a re-render that clears every clause of his reason ships with whatever is left named on the card. Holding a nearly-clean render back because the same class is still faintly present is how a slot spends four renders on one clause.
- **A different never-approved class that arrived with the fix.** The bullet above is about the *same* defect shrinking; a render that answers his reason completely and independently grows an invented text surface, a duplicated subject or a pasted block is a fresh hold-back, not a residual. Clearing the clause tells you what the next prompt must answer, not what this frame may carry in front of him — judgement is the whole frame, each time. Two hold-backs inside one round are legitimate when they fail on different classes: each costs one render, while a card carrying a class the library has never approved spends the round and leaves the slot with nothing to show.

- **A brief's check item that the post never states is the prompt's own invention, not a requirement.** A brief is written by the run that wrote the prompt, so its check list inherits prompt details — a tally count, a lid position, a prop's exact proportion — and reads them as if the section demanded them. Grep the post for the detail before treating a miss as a defect: when the prose carries no count or no such prop, a render one short of it ships with the miss named in his terms and the reason it does not matter (`the stick carries three notches where the prompt asked for four; the post states no count, so the beat reads either way`). A brief clause with no basis in the post buys a re-render for nothing, and the same clause read as a hard requirement is how a clean render gets held back. **The mirror case is a brief's residual that is not there at all:** a brief listed `no hinged lid on the bin` for a render whose bin carries a lid at the rim, and copying it would have put a false statement about the picture on a frozen card. Check every residual on a crop of its own object before it reaches an item description, and drop the ones the picture contradicts — he reads the frame against your sentence, so a residual you did not measure costs you his trust rather than buying leniency.

  **The same rule runs the other way for a brief's positive claim.** A brief can assert that a crop already cleared a render (`the crop of x560-1000 y330-720 settled it: key in the lock, padlock reads open`) and write that cleared state straight into the item's check note. Re-running that crop, then a 5x crop of the object itself, showed the opposite — empty keyhole, shackle seated, cloth draped over the lock face — so copying the claim would have put a beat on the card that the frame does not carry. Re-measure every assertion a brief makes about the picture, the ones that clear it as well as the ones that condemn it, before it reaches an item description. A brief is one run's reading of a PNG, not a measurement.

  **The same distrust applies to a brief's quotation of the draft, not only to its reading of a PNG.** A card brief that quotes the post's embed as `![](assets/…)` and calls it "the file does not exist, no alt text" is the brief writer's shorthand for the missing file; the draft's real line often carries a word (`![flux-reconcile](assets/…)`). Everything downstream hangs off that line — the closing pass rewrites its alt text from the picture that ships, and a card whose details tell him the embed has no alt text is describing a file he can open. Grep the post for the asset path, quote the line as it is, and say in the closing comment that it already carried a word.

## The comparison engine's drift is the comparison's finding, not a hold-back

When a round ships an ideogram render and a flux companion of the same scene, the companion answers a different question — *what does the other engine make of this composition?* — and the ideogram is what goes in the post. So a companion's own drift is a clause on its card item, not a reason to hold the round:

- **flux2-klein paints small green containers into the background under a prompt that bans green everywhere.** Three companions of one round, each written with the palette named in words and `nothing green anywhere` in its opening line, came back with a green jar, a green bin and a greenish-blue canister on a shelf or under a bench — background clutter, none of it in the beat. Expect it, crop to confirm the green sits off the beat, and put it in that companion's check note as one clause (`three muted green containers on the right-hand shelf — the palette clause's one visible miss`). The drift is informative rather than fatal: it is part of what the pair of renders exists to show him.

**A green-share count against an approved sibling from the same week separates drift from a series breach, and the blob's size is not the test.** Run `check-green-share.py` on the candidate and on a frame he approved that week: an approved frame reads 0.1-0.5% green with no blob of 2000 px or more, while a render whose *cast* is the old round Android-mascot body reads 6-27% with blobs sitting exactly on the androids. Green clutter beside the beat is a clause; green on the android is the cast, and every series record describes the helper as a knee-high boxy grey body taken from the scene's own palette, so a green mascot is a hold-back however the prompt was worded — including when the prompt for it was written by the run that also quoted the palette rule. **When every shipping slot of a round carries that one class, the round is a render round and no card opens at submit time:** one comment carries the measured shares, the failing class and the new job ids, the arrival is stated as an absolute time rather than "about twenty minutes" because the replacements join the back of a shared queue, and the card is built only from the run where the replacements pass. This is the case the drift bullets above do not cover: a class he rejects whole frames over is never "a residual named on the card", and a card that names it spends the round and leaves the slot with nothing.
- **A correction submitted after the round's batch is not a correction you can show.** A replacement companion written for a mark that small joins the queue behind every job submitted after the batch and can read tens of hours out, so it becomes the next round's attempt instead. Ship the draft with the clause named and put the replacement's job id in the brief; the arithmetic in *Budget against the queue* is the same one, and it rarely justifies waiting.
- **A background sticker whose mark is a couple of abstract dashes is not the `unreadable-text` hold-back.** The test is whether a reader takes the surface for something written — a sheet of paragraph blocks with margins and a signature line is, and it holds; a small plate on a machine in the back corner carrying two dashes and a rough U is not, and it ships with the clause. Both engines produce that plate, and an approval history that already accepted a header carrying the same dash-line label settles it. **The same call holds when the marks are everywhere but only note-sized.** A cork board covered in sticky notes whose marks are horizontal dashes standing in for writing is that shorthand, not pseudo-text, even though the prompt asked for blank notes: settle it with one crop of the board at about 3x, which shows strokes and no letterform, and say in the item description that the notes carry greeking dashes and spell nothing. What makes a surface the `unreadable-text` class is a mark a reader takes for a word — paragraph blocks with margins, a signature line, letterforms that resolve — never an illustration's convention for ink at prop scale.
- **A companion that fails its own beat twice gets a written prompt, not a third render.** When the companion's whole point is a prop match — two plates that must be identical, a door that must read open — the engine drifts on the small prop and the frame says something else, so it is a beat failure and is held back like any other; it is not shipped as `drift`. The second failure of the same clause is evidence the prompt is wrong, not the model: write the correction as its own prompt file for the slot, stating the prop once with a fixed shape and count and then calling the second copy `the same plate again`, note on the card that the corrected file waits for the next batch, and do not spend a render on a comparison image behind a deep queue. **The exception is a beat that is a structure inside an object, where there is nothing to correct because the engine class is the limit** — nested rings in a cross-section, a snapped bracket against an intact one, a closed loop that has to be counted. On one round all three flux companions lost exactly that part of the beat (a cut onion came back with one smooth inner face, a collapsed shelf came back level with both brackets, a three-crate loop came back as two crates on parallel rails) while the ideograms on the same prompts drew them. Do not rewrite those companions: ship the ideograms, close the flux half as a class limit, name it on the card, and pair the flux twin with a pose beat — a bound prop, a hatch with light under it, a bell without its clapper — which is what flux does draw. The rule and the measurements are in `prompt-hygiene.md`.

- **A direction beat is a second engine limit of the same kind as the raised hand: flux2-klein puts the pushing figure on the far side of the load.** A companion whose point is that a copy travels *into* a machine — a cart pushed ahead of the android toward an open hatch — came back twice with the android standing between the cart and the machine, one hand on the handle, so the picture says the load is being taken away. The second prompt stated the layout as an ordered walk (`at the centre the cart ... at the right of the cart the android, behind its handle, pushing it ahead of itself toward the right edge, and at the far right edge the drum`), and the render put the same three objects in the same wrong order. Check the direction by **ordering, never by asking `is it pushing toward the drum?`** — that question returns yes, because it offers the model the answer inside itself. Ask instead for a positional list (`list each object with its centre as a fraction of the frame width`) and read the sequence: `cart 0.54 < android 0.63 < drum 0.89` means the android is between the load and its target, so the beat is reversed however convincing the arms look. When the same direction clause fails twice, the companion is closed rather than re-prompted a third time: the slot ships the ideogram alone, the card's `detailsMarkdown` says the flux comparison is **closed**, not queued, and names the two attempts, and both files stay in staging uncollected. A spare that fails this way was never on a card, so it gets no library record — naming it in the queue note and on the issue is the whole trail.

## The arithmetic that decides the close calls

A slot's render budget is part of the judgement, not only the defect's class. **Rounds and renders are different counters**: rounds are cards, and the ceiling is three per request; renders-per-round is not capped, but a slot that has spent three renders inside one round has spent more than the card will hold attention for, and a fourth render on a small non-story artifact is worth less than the artifact it removes. When the two pull against each other — a defect class he rejects, versus a slot already deep into its render budget — measure the artifact's size and its distance from the beat (a crop to disk, not a read) and ship it named if the beat survives.

## Budget against the queue, not against the batch cap

A render that lands after the owner's review window is worth nothing to the campaign, so the number that decides whether to spend a slot is `queue_ahead` from `imagegen status <job>`, not the six-slot cap. On a shared box carrying two parallel runners' batches, twenty jobs ahead turns one ideogram into about ten hours — past the point where any card will be read that session.

- **A post with more slots than the cap allows is three paired slots plus a deferral, not four single-engine ones.** A draft can carry four or more image slots while the six-render cap covers three of them at both engines. Pair the slots the post's argument runs through — the header, the slot whose section is the turn of the post, the slot carrying the contrast the post exists for — and leave the detail slot for round 2, named in the brief and in the queue note with the reason it went second. Four ideogram-only slots buys breadth and loses the comparison the owner asked for on every regeneration, and a comparison is what he rules on; three honest pairs keep the card shape he already answers.
- **Read `queue_ahead` before submitting, and shrink the batch when the queue is deep.** The cap governs one run; the box is governed by what every runner submits together. Fewer slots per run beats a bigger batch every time the queue is long.
- **A two-slot repair may ride behind a full six-render batch, but only when the lane holds nothing but that batch.** Check the job table first: if every `pending`/`running` row is your own, the repair pushes nobody else's card back — but **it does not ride for free**, and the "under an hour of tail" this rule used to promise is wrong by measurement: a header ideogram created at 19:56Z behind six of the run's own jobs only *started* at 22:50Z, three hours later, because an ideogram takes 53–73 minutes on this box and a flux frame only 3–7. A repair submitted behind a batch therefore arrives a whole batch's render time late, and if the batch's card is waiting on its own collect the two collide. So treat a repair behind a batch as a deliberate three-hour delay, not a rounding error: submit it only when the batch is nearly through its queue, otherwise leave the linted prompt on disk for the next wake. If any sibling runner's job is queued, hold it — that is the case the cap exists for. Either way, say on the issue how many renders that makes in the lane against the six-slot convention and why, and offer to stop doing it: an overage disclosed as a decision is reviewable, and the same overage left silent reads as carelessness.
- **A slot may ship on one engine when the other engine is queued.** If one engine's render clears the bar and the other's is stuck behind hours of work, put the passing one on the card and name the missing engine as queued on the card itself. Holding a whole post for an engine comparison costs the post its place in the queue; his verdict on the engine that landed is still worth having.
- **Hop the monitor, do not reach for the ETA.** A `nextCheckAt` set many hours out is silently dropped, so a long wait never wakes you. Schedule roughly two hours ahead and re-arm on each wake until the job is actually `done`, and re-read `monitorNextCheckAt` after the write that set it. **A job the brief says is hours out but which is already `running` takes its own measured duration plus ten minutes instead, and that duration is engine-specific** — a flux frame is 3–7 minutes and `eta_minutes` is close, while an ideogram4 render has been measured at 53 to 93 minutes on the same box, so a wake armed at `eta_minutes + 10` for an ideogram fires while it is still pending and the hop is spent reading the job table. Take the duration from the engine (`running` on an ideogram: arm about an hour out) rather than from the number the status prints. — the two-hour hop is for a job still in the queue. On a slot whose known failure class is hands, catching a bad render at its own ETA puts the rewritten prompt into the queue *behind the sibling job still rendering*, so the re-render runs alongside the pair instead of costing a second wait afterwards; a two-hour hop spends that window waiting for nothing.
- **`eta_minutes` on a `running` job is the render's nominal duration, not the time left.** It prints the same number minute after minute while the job works — a job that has said `21` for an hour is rendering, not stalled — so compute arrival from the moment the status flipped to `running` plus that duration, and re-poll rather than trusting the number to count down. Once the wait is shorter than the job's own duration, spend it inside the run instead of paying a wake for it: `sleep` in chunks under the foreground command cap (a foreground command here is cut at about seven minutes, so keep each sleep under six), poll between them, and fetch each file as it lands — the next wake then has only the card left to build.
- **Re-read `queue_ahead` at every wake instead of carrying the ETA forward.** Under a batch the queue moves in both directions: a set of jobs read at submit as six hours out were mostly finished an hour into the wait, and a job submitted a few minutes later read twelve hours out because a whole sibling batch had landed behind it. So an ETA is a snapshot of the jobs ahead at that instant, not a time the render arrives, and the arithmetic that decides a hold-back has to be redone from a fresh `queue_ahead` rather than from the number in the closing comment.
- **Read the queue without flooding the run with prompts.** `imagegen status` takes no `--json` and prints the whole record including the render's full prompt text, so one call per job over six jobs dumps kilobytes of prompt into the context. `imagegen status <job> | grep -E '"(status|queue_ahead|eta_minutes)"' | tr -d ' \n'` gives the three numbers on one line, and the same command in a `for` loop prints the whole set.
- **Read `queue_ahead` before pulling a live card, because the replacements join the same queue.** Withdrawing a posted card is still the right move for a bar defect — a fused hand must not sit in front of him — but the re-renders land behind every job already queued, so on a busy box the correction is ten hours out and the round stalls for most of a day. Read the number first and put the expected wait in the comment that goes with the withdrawal, so the owner can plan around it instead of finding a card missing. A slot that passed in the pulled round carries over: its attachment is already on the issue and the corrected card points at it again, so only the failing slots are re-rendered.

A held-back render is not free and it is not silent. It gets its own library record the moment it is held back, `asset: null`, with a `rejected` row whose reason says the hold-back was the self-check's and carries the crops that showed the defect. Never write a rejection in the owner's voice for a render he never saw — that record reads back later as a rule about his taste.

**A round built only from self-check hold-backs is not a round he rejected, and the handoff and the card have to say which it is.** The handoff template's first line reads `re-renders of the images Wojtek rejected on <card>`, which is false when every attempt of the slot died at your own check — write `the <slot> slot, held back at my own self-check through round 1 and never shown` instead. In that card's `detailsMarkdown`, the standing `no draft image is on the card` sentence becomes the truth that the post still carries the old image and that approving this render is what replaces it. `backlog.py ready <n> <k> <brief>` queues the round exactly as a rejected one does; the runner opens the card. Two things belong in that handoff because the next run cannot see the staging folder or the earlier attempts: the slot's whole chain by stem (the back-filled record and every held-back attempt) and each attempt's one-line failure, so the next card can name the history instead of re-deriving it from the queue notes.

## The duplication case, because it costs two renders if missed

A one-figure picture comes back with two identical people when the prompt asks for one figure **by naming the duplicate**. `no second person`, `no twin`, `no copy of him anywhere in the frame` hands the model that shape, and repeating it makes the shape likelier; a tight crop of a lone figure in open space invites a companion. Two consecutive attempts can fail this way, and the fix that works is not a better-worded ban:

1. State the cast **positively and once** — the man is the whole subject of the shot, the pavement at his feet described as bare.
2. Carry **no** twin/copy/duplicate/second-person clause at all.
3. Change the composition so a duplicate has nowhere to stand — a portrait from the knees up that fills the frame, not a figure standing in open space.

Point 3 is what separates the attempt that works from the two that do not: the first two attempts reworded the same ban and both duplicated. The same mechanism governs any prop the prompt forbids by name — a prohibition is read as a description.

**The other route to a duplicate is a pose split across one element's bbox.** A prompt that puts two arm actions inside a single figure element whose box spans only one of the arms leaves the raised arm outside the box, and the model fills that space with a second body: a rotation slot's two keys came back on two identical men, one lowering the old key over the bucket and one raising the new. The repair is geometric, not verbal. Give the figure one box that spans both hands, say the arms come from his one pair of shoulders, and fold any prop a hand holds into the person's element so a standalone prop element cannot seed a figure of its own. `check-bboxes.py <prompt>` prints every box as `y a-b, x c-d` in the schema's `[y1, x1, y2, x2]` order — read it to see whether both arms actually fit inside the person's box before touching the wording.

**The member that gets duplicated is usually the helper, not the man, because the count clause names only the man.** A header whose cast is the chibi man plus one knee-high grey android came back with **two androids**, and the prompt's cast clause read `one person only, no second person, no crowd` — the human was counted and the helper never was, so the model had a spare body to spend and spent it on the member nobody had constrained. Count every cast member the series allows in the same positive sentence (`one man at the bench and one knee-high grey android at his elbow`), and weigh it: a picture whose point is one helper doing one job loses that point to a second body, so a second android is a `wrong-count` hold-back even though an extra undifferentiated body in a background would be a note. The geometry in point 3 repairs it identically: a tightly framed version of the same scene, the man cut by the frame's bottom edge and the android half cut by its corner, came back from the other engine with a single android. **A head count is positional evidence, not an impression.** Settle a suspected duplicate with two overlapping crops at about 2x that each return one complete face at a different coordinate, plus a reduced whole-frame read (`crop-probe.py <png> --box 0,0,1216,832 --scale 0.55`): a face at x≈350 and another at x≈715 inside overlapping bands means two heads, while one face centred in both bands means one. A single full-frame read is the reason to crop, never the finding.

**A read that volunteers the picture's provenance is confabulating, and everything else it says is unverified.** Twice in one batch a crop read opened with a confident identification — naming a feature film and its director for one render, a named game and a walkthrough video for another — and then answered the question from that invented context: it reported spray and mist around the paddles of a wheel whose channel bed, cut at 3x, is dry stone under dust, and it reported a third hand that a whole-figure crop did not contain. Treat a source-credit sentence as the tell that the read is describing a remembered image rather than this file, discard its findings, and settle the claim on a crop of the surface or part it was asked about. The same suspicion applies to a read that answers in the shape of the question (spray where the prompt described water): ask for a positional or measurable answer instead of a yes/no.

**And the negative cast clause stays wrong even when the defect is a duplicate.** The instinct after a second body appears is to write `nobody beside him` or `there is no second person in the room`; that is exactly the ban this section rules out, and it raises the odds of a third. Fix the box and the pose, say the cast once, and let the frame's emptiness do the rest.

**This file governs over the "an extra figure is a quirk" reading of the hold-back bullet in `SKILL.md` step 6.** That line is right for a body the beat does not depend on, and wrong for a copy of the protagonist.

## A hand fused into the prop it grips is a hold-back, and writing the defect on the card does not make it shippable

- **A hand whose fingers melt into the prop it holds is a hold-back, not a residual to name.** Post 052's round 2 shipped two renders with the defect written into their item descriptions as `Flagged:` (`the hand on it is a fused mitten`, `six digits on the open palm`), on the reasoning that the beat still read. Naming a show-bar clause on a frozen card is not a fix and not a licence: the bar's `figures correct` clause is the one he rejects on sight, and a card carrying it spends his attention and the round. The self-check's own note said the hand was broken, which is the whole decision.
- **The cause is the pose, so the fix is the composition, never an adjective.** Both prompts had said two hands onto one small prop (`both fists grip the round bow`, `both palms holding one short key flat between them`), which is the shape this file's other sections already rule out. The repair that keeps the beat: stand the prop in its socket, on the cradle or flat on the bench, and hang both hands empty at his sides, each one `a simple rounded shape with a thumb bump, touching nothing`. The key that is too broad for the slot is carried by the slot, the door that stands open is carried by its own lock; neither needs a hand on it.
- **Decide it on a 2x crop of each hand, written to disk, before the card goes up.** A full-frame read of the same frames called the fused mitten `roughly four visible fingers` and the six-digit palm `a hand with a slight distortion`, so the full frame is the reason to crop and never the finding. Two reads that agree on the defect hold the render back; two that agree it is clean (the device render's five digits, three reads) let it ship. **Agreement counts only when both reads sit on correctly framed, pixel-exact cuts.** Two readers called a drawn fist a `fused mitten` on crops cut from guessed coordinates and upscaled with LANCZOS, which softens one-pixel ink lines until a drawn finger separation reads as a smooth edge; the same frames re-cut nearest-neighbour at 8x from a box located by fractions showed separated fingers and a thumb along the grip, again twice. An eye-placed crop manufactures the defect it is looking for, and a hold-back written off one costs a render and a round for a clean picture.

## An absent hand is a hold-back; a four-digit grip is a style shortcut

- **An arm that ends in a stump with no hand at all is a hold-back**, whatever the brief says the render was submitted for. It reads as an amputee, so the picture says something its section does not. Settle it with two readers on crops at 3x and 6x of the arm's end, not with a full-frame read: `crop-probe.py <png> --box x0,y0,x1,y1 --scale 3 --out crop.png` then read that file, and repeat with `vread.py <png> "…" --box` (a second, different reader). A brief that names one caveat for a render ("the twig floats beside the hand") has not thereby checked the hands, so its caveat is not a clearance.
- **"Arms run down behind his back so no hand is drawn at all" trades a fused hand for an amputated arm, and the ideogram engine is the one that does it.** The pose reads as the safest repair in the book — nothing on the body to mangle — and a header re-rendered under exactly that sentence came back with the visible arm ending in a rounded stump, sleeve clean, no forearm, no wrist, no hand. Hiding a hand from the model's view does not delete the limb from its geometry; the arm is still drawn and now has no end to draw. So the hands-hidden pose is a last resort, not the replacement for a failed fist.
- **The pose family that actually passes, in order:** both fists resting flat on a real surface that is described in its own element (a desk edge, a crate rim, a bench top, the seat of a stool), then hands hanging straight down at his sides with nothing near them, then the figure out of the frame entirely. When a slot has already failed one hand pose, go to a flat surface rather than to hiding; when it has failed two — including a stump — stop reposing the man and cut him from the picture, because the beat in every case was carried by the object, not by him.
- **Locate the part before you cut it: a grid-overlay read of pixel bounds is a guess wearing a measurement's clothes.** Asking a reader for a hand's coordinates off a labelled grid returned boxes that landed on the door frame and on bare wood, so the 8x nearest cuts came back `no hand present in this crop` — that is evidence the box is wrong, never evidence about the hand, and a hold-back written off it is a hold-back written off nothing. Cut one wide crop of the whole figure at about 2x and count the digits there in context; cut a tight box only if one hand still needs settling, and take its position as fractions of the wide crop rather than as absolute pixels from a reader. **A read that returns an empty completion with `finish_reason=length` has spent its budget reasoning and given you no answer:** re-asking the same route, even with a one-line question, can come back empty again, so treat that read as absent, do not loop on it, and settle the count on the 2x crop through the run's main reader.
- **A closed chibi fist is drawn as a rounded mitten with no separately drawn thumb, and that is the style.** Both hands of one approved frame read on 8x crops as three to four knuckle bumps with no distinct thumb, and the fists still read as fists; at 1216 px the defect signal on a fist is an extra shape, a second limb or a gap where a hand should be, never the absence of an anatomical thumb. A read that answers `anatomically implausible` has answered a question about anatomy the series was never drawing, so do not hold a render back for it.
- **A pinching or gripping hand drawn as a thumb plus three fingers is a 2D cartoon convention and ships with one clause naming it.** Both of two readers call it a normal stylistic shortening, nothing is occluded and the grip still reads as a grip. The test stays "what does the picture say": a missing little finger inside a fist that reads as a fist is a style mark, while a hand missing from the wrist is a defect. Counting digits on a crop is still the way to decide which one is in front of you, and the clause goes in the item description so he can rule on it. **Frame that crop on the hand alone and keep the prop out of the box:** with a key's bow inside the frame the bow and the top finger merge, and the same fist reads thumb plus three at 2.5x where a 5x crop of the hand by itself shows four fingers and a thumb. A count that disagrees between two crops is a framing finding until both are cut on the hand only.
- **A palm pressed flat with the fingers spread is the one pose where a correct five-digit hand reads as six.** The thumb lies in the same plane as the fingers, so a reader answering "count the fingertips in a row" folds it into the row and reports an extra digit: three tight crops of one palm flat on a glass panel each came back `six digits, high confidence`. Before a flat-palm count can hold a render back, ask the reader to name the thumb separately from the row, or count on a crop framed on the palm by itself — the same hand drawn with a fist or a grip reads correctly on the same treatment. A hold-back written off a folded-in thumb costs a render and a round for a correct hand.
- **A "detached fleshy shape" on a hand gripping a prop is usually the reader, and both engines collect the same sentence.** An ideogram and a flux render of one slot — two different drawings — each came back with `an anomalous detached fleshy shape near the hand` on nearest-neighbour crops that included the handle and the hand's edge: a thumb folded along the grip sits one ink gap away from the fist, and the reader names that gap. Before a hold-back is written off a grip, cut the hand alone with the handle out of the box and read the ink mask (`crop-nearest.py --ink`): one continuous contour plus a single crease is a mitten fist (the style, ships with the clause), while separate finger lines appear only where fingers are drawn. Two reads that cannot even place the handle inside the fist are describing their own crop framing, not the drawing.
- **Locate a part with a 1:1 figure crop and fractions, never with a reader's absolute pixel box.** Three boxes asked of one render landed on the wall, on the torso and on bare bench, and the crops cut from them answered "no hand present" — evidence about the box, not the hand. Cut the whole figure at 1:1, ask for the part's centre as fractions of that crop, map the fractions back to original pixels, then cut at 6-8x.

## A style clause is a composition instruction, and a bad one flattens the frame

- **A finish described as a layer over the whole picture makes flux polished, and the owner will call it that.** Asking for a 1990s cel "printed on paper and then photographed", with an even film grain over walls, floor and empty air alike, paper fibre, soft halation and aged colour, tells the model to be uniform everywhere — and uniformity is exactly what the eye reads as a clean digital render. He then reports the symptom ("too flat, too polished, missing details") and not the clause, so the fix is to find the clause rather than to add more detail nouns.
- **Write the drawn-line half positively, and leave the grain as a light finish on the sheet.** Tapered inked contours with a slight hand wobble, shadows built from visible hatching and cross-hatching instead of airbrush, individual strokes in hair and cloth, flat cel colour laid slightly off-register so paper shows at each colour edge, halftone dots in the half-shadows, plus no glow, no blur, no vector finish. That clause moved flux measurably closer to the ideogram linework he prefers, while still reading smoother than the ideogram under it.
- **Flux2-klein cannot draw a prop held up in a hand, so budget for it.** Five attempts at one header drew the raised hand as a closed fist with the little finger merged into the block, under prompts that asked for an open hand, five separately drawn fingers and a key held by its bow. Naming the hand harder does not fix it; an ideogram render of the same composition draws it cleanly first time. When a slot's beat needs a hand presenting an object and the engine is flux, expect a hold-back and say so before the round starts rather than after four renders.
- **A finish judgement taken from a crop read is not evidence, and asking a reader to compare a candidate against a frame he already approved does not make it one.** Asked whether a fresh frame carried inked contours and hatching, a reader answered `no — painterly, value-based, no line art`; the identical question put to a crop of the already-approved frame from the same post came back with the same answer. At 2x neither answer describes anything a viewer notices at card size, so a `different medium` verdict cannot decide between ship and hold — and holding on it costs a render for a frame the owner would likely have taken. Decide finish on the edge scripts plus whether the beat reads, and act on a finish complaint only when the owner names one in his own words. The bar's clause about a different idiom covers a pasted element inside one frame, never the question of whether one image matches its siblings.
- **Prohibitions by name drift the rest of the frame.** "One key in the whole picture" produced a second key on the desk in three attempts, "no plaque" produced a blank plaque where the shelf belonged, and "no label" produced a barcode on the cardboard. State the positive inventory and change nothing else; count the props that matter on a crop before deciding the drift is acceptable.

## The second engine is not a second sitting, and one slot does not get its own card

- **A passing spare waits in staging; it does not get a card of its own.** When a slot's re-render passes but that slot is already on a card awaiting the owner's verdict, do not open a second card to show the other engine. One post is one sitting, and a follow-up card carrying a single slot of a post he has not ruled on yet spends a second interruption on a comparison he did not ask for. Put the staging path, byte size and sha256 in the queue note and in the issue comment, and card it only if his verdict rejects the engine that is on the card — at which point the replacement is already rendered instead of newly queued into a deep shared queue. **A job still in the queue when the card closes has no wake path: a Paperclip monitor fires only on an issue that is `in_progress` or `in_review`, so marking the card `done` kills the monitor that was going to collect it.** Do not imply in the closing comment that it will be picked up — name the job id, say plainly that the slot is already decided and that the frame will stay on the box uncollected unless someone asks for it, and put the same line in the queue note so a later round on that post knows the render exists. Holding costs nothing: on this campaign a held-back header was exactly the render the post shipped once the verdict went against the engine on the card, and a spare that is never needed is one file in staging.
- **A flux hand defect is evidence about the engine, not about the slot, while the ideogram is still rendering.** When both engines are queued for one slot and the companion lands first with a fused grip, check the main engine before spending the slot's one silent re-render: ideogram draws a hand on a prop cleanly far more often than flux — this file's other sections reach the same conclusion for a prop held up in a hand — so the ideogram may already clear the bar and a re-render written off the companion pays twice for one question. Only when the main engine fails too does the slot spend its render, and the fix is still a pose change, not an adjective.

- **A card with holes plus a second card later is two sittings; wait while the renders are already paid for.** Partial sets are tempting when two of three slots have a passing render and the third slot's re-renders are already in the queue, but nothing in the campaign requires a card the moment one slot is ready — the definition of done accepts "a re-render in flight with a monitor". If the outstanding jobs land within a few hours, card once at the last ETA: the difference is one evening against two interruptions of the same post, and the second card arrives after he has already spent attention on the first. Re-check the ETAs before committing to the wait, because a deep shared queue stretches from five hours to twelve between wakes and a wait that long is worse than the partial card.
- **The exception is a missing slot with nothing left in flight: card what passes and put that slot on a later card of its own.** The wait above is a wait for renders already paid for. When the missing slot has spent its attempts and the next step is a **new prompt object** — a state the engine cannot draw at all, five renders deep — there is nothing to wait for, and holding three clean images for it costs the owner a day for a picture that does not exist yet. Open the card, and state in `detailsMarkdown`, in one paragraph: which slot is absent, how many attempts it spent, what each failed on, and that a replacement object is already in the lane with its job id. Then card that slot alone when it lands. A post cannot ship without its header, so the second card is coming either way; the only choice is whether he sees the rest this afternoon. **Say it as absent, never as a caveat on a similar frame** — the reason the slot is missing is that its candidate says the opposite of the section, and offering that frame as `the header, with a note` is the same defect with a paragraph attached.
- **Say whether the missing engine is queued or failed, because they are different facts to the owner.** "the flux companion is still in the queue" and "the flux companion failed the hand check and is not shown" read the same on a card and mean opposite things about whether a comparison is still coming. When the second engine fails the bar rather than lagging, the slot ships one engine only, and the item says which clause its twin failed. **And the same sentence the other way round when the main engine is what failed:** if the ideogram was held back for a show-bar class and its companion passed, the companion is the slot's item, its description says in one clause that it is the companion (`flux render, because the ideogram drew two androids`), and the corrected ideogram's job id is named on the card with the promise to say so if it lands and beats this one. That keeps one verdict per slot without holding a whole post behind a deep queue for an engine the owner can rule on later.

  **A companion that is the slot's only item becomes the slot's asset when he approves it, so the item has to say what a yes means.** Earlier guidance here said a companion can never become the post's asset and the slot should be left off the card instead; a verdict answered that — a card whose header slot carried the flux companion, described in one clause as `flux render, because the ideogram drew two androids` and naming the corrected ideogram's job id, came back approved on all three items, and the flux frame is the post's header. So the card may offer the companion as the candidate, and the approval is read as the verdict on the picture that ships — which means the item's description has to be explicit that it is the companion, why the main engine is absent (a show-bar class, not a queue wait), and which job id holds the corrected main render. Then the closing does what any approved render does: copy the staging PNG to `tech_blog/assets/<today>/<item id>-r<k>.png`, repoint that one embed and rewrite its alt text to describe the new picture, write the record with the approved row, set `superseded_by` on the slot's earlier record to the approved job, and give the held-back main render its own `asset: null` record whose reason is the self-check's. Leave the slot off the card and name it in `detailsMarkdown` with its job ids only when no render for it clears the bar at all — a companion that passes is a shippable candidate, not a comparison that cannot ship.

## A script's pale-band flag is settled by a column profile, not by a second read

`check-blank-margin.py` reports a fill share and any row or column band at 80% fill or more, and a painted pale wall, a paper-cream sky or a white backdrop answers it with no defect in the frame: one flux render of a storeroom doorway read a 35% fill share with a column band across the right 300 px, and that band was the exterior wall.

A vision read of the same corner is not a tie-breaker. Two reads of one corner came back **opposite** — *an empty paper margin outside a panel border* and *full-bleed painted wall with texture and scuffs* — and the first had invented a border that appears nowhere in the file. A disagreement between two reads is evidence about the reader, not about the pixels, so it settles nothing in either direction.

Measure it, because "is there ink here" is countable. `scripts/check-pale-band.py <png> --columns 912,1216` prints the band's mean, standard deviation and dark-pixel share, then the dark share per sampled column. Ink and hatching leave about **9–15% dark pixels per column with a spread well above 40**; an unrendered band is a flat fill with near-zero dark pixels and a spread under about 10. On that frame the right 300 px ran 9–15% dark with sd 56 all the way to the last pixel column, so the wall was painted and the flag was scenery.

So a fill-share flag on a pale region is a prompt to measure, never a defect and never a clearance: run the profile, put the numbers in the note (band share, sd, the per-column range), and let the band's own ink decide. The same probe answers the mirrored case — a pale band the render really did leave blank is usually the one region where the model had nothing to draw, and the profile shows it as a flat fill from edge to edge with no column breaking 1%.

**Read the `blank strips:` line before the share, because a night scene fills a fifth of the frame with one dark colour and no defect.** A night exterior printed a 23% fill share with `blank strips: top 0 bottom 0 left 0 right 0` — the share was the cold sky above and the shadow under the wall, and the frame was clean. The strips line is the part that matters: zeros at all four edges at the script's 98% threshold mean nothing reaches the border, so no probe is owed. A share is a prompt to look, and the column profile is only worth running when the same report also names a row or column band at its fill threshold.

**The mirrored case is a dark band the render really did leave, and the profile numbers do not clear it.** A scene that stops at row 724 of 832 and leaves 108 px of near-black beneath it reads as noise on paper (600–900 distinct colours per row) and still reads as an empty strip to a viewer, which is the only verdict that matters for a picture going in a post. The cause is usually the prompt: a shadow named by location (`deep indigo shadow at the near edge of the bench`) is painted as a bar along that edge. The rule and its repair are in `prompt-hygiene.md` under *a lighting clause can paint a bar across the frame* — hold the frame, say the boards run to the last row of pixels, and put a described object in each near corner.

## A hop with nothing to fetch is a hop for the live-post recheck

A wake that finds every job still `pending` has an hour of real work in it that costs no render: the campaign's definition of done is about the posts, not about the queue, and the queue is the only thing a hop looks at by default.

- **Re-run `backlog/audit/recheck-live-posts.py` and quote its number, not an older one.** It walks every post not `done` in the queue, resolves each `![...](...)` embed against the vault, and runs `check-grey-bars` and `check-palette-strip` on every distinct file — about a minute, no model. A sweep of the assets folder counts files that exist and says nothing about what a reviewer sees; this counts what the drafts actually embed. Report the delta against the last reading and what caused it (a card approved replaces its slot), so the number reads as progress rather than as drift.
- **Cross-check every flagged embed against that post's open card, endpoint by endpoint, never by assumption.** A flagged file whose slot is on the card clears on approval; a flagged file whose card does not carry that slot is a live hazard, and it is the one defect this campaign can still ship by accident.
- **A card still being built will not have that check done for it.** A card that is `in_progress` with no attachments and no pending verdict request has not chosen its items yet, and the run building it audits the draft for nothing — so a flagged embed there reaches his eye as an approved post image. Comment on that card: the embed path, which check it fails, and the two clean options (put a round-2 render for that slot on the card, or state on the card that the post still ships the image), plus the recheck command so the claim is checkable without trusting you. **Never edit, PATCH or withdraw another run's card.** It is their slot; the comment is the whole intervention, and it is enough because the finding is mechanical.
- **Re-read `queue.json` inside the run that writes the comment.** Under a shared workspace a post moves between `card` and `rendering` mid-hop — a card closing and a fresh round-2 card opening, from a runner you cannot see — so a state list printed ten minutes earlier is already stale, and `backlog.py next` plus the per-state counts all come from that one file, which the next runner reads back.

## A long overnight campaign runs out of monitor attempts, and takes the comment with it

A monitor PATCH answering `422 {"error":"Monitor bounds are already exhausted","details":{"clearReason":"max_attempts_exhausted"}}` means the **attempt counter**, not the clock, ended the chain — and because monitor and comment ride one request, the issue comment bundled with it is **not posted either**. A run can therefore lose its whole report and leave no trace of the failure on the issue.

Size the bounds by the work: an overnight campaign that hops every two hours needs `maxAttempts` 20-40, and `timeoutAt` has to sit past the last expected render (a 23:30Z bound on a queue that talks about 05:00Z kills the chain before the render lands). Re-send the whole PATCH with a larger `maxAttempts` and a later `timeoutAt`, then confirm both the comment count and `monitorNextCheckAt` afterwards — the failed attempt leaves neither.

**The monitor note itself is capped at 500 characters, and that rejection takes the bundled comment with it.** `PATCH /issues/<id>` with an `executionPolicy.monitor.notes` longer than 500 answers `400 Validation error … ["executionPolicy","monitor","notes"] … too_big`, and because monitor and comment ride one request the write is lost whole: no monitor, no comment, nothing on the issue to show it happened. Keep the note to a couple of job ids and one instruction, put the long form in the comment body, and re-read `monitorNextCheckAt` after every arm — a 200 response is the only proof either landed.

**Arm past the last ETA you are actually waiting on, and re-arm rather than trusting the first estimate.** An ETA is a snapshot of the queue at submit time and other runners keep adding work behind it, so a wake armed at the first number often fires before the render exists and the hop is spent re-reading `pending`. Read `queue_ahead` on each job at the hop and set `nextCheckAt` past the last one in the set; a wake that arrives early is cheap to move but it costs a whole run.

## The card-versus-draft coverage check is a script now, and it reads items off the interaction

The flagged-embed-versus-open-card cross-check is mechanical, so it lives in `~/image-studio/backlog/audit/card-embed-coverage.py`: for every post whose queue state is `card` (or `rendering` with `--rendering`) it resolves the draft's embeds, runs `check-grey-bars` and `check-palette-strip` on each, and asks whether the post's open card carries an item naming that slot. An embed that fails a check with no item pointing at it is the defect that ships by omission. Run it before a card goes up and again on a hop that has nothing to fetch.

Two details cost a wrong first run, both worth knowing before re-reading the output:

- **A card's items are at `payload.items` of its latest `request_item_verdicts` interaction**, each with `id`, `label`, `description` and `attachmentId`. `issue.items` and `issue.attachments` on the issue object are `null`; attachments need `GET /issues/<id>/attachments`, which also gives `originalFilename` and `sha256`.
- **A post in flight carries its issue on `queue.json`'s `slot`, not in the `batch` list**, so a script reading only `batch` reports the busiest post as having no card at all and flags every embed on it.

Matching is token overlap between the item id and the embed filename, so a hit is a pointer and a miss is what gets looked at; do not read a hit as proof the slot is covered. A card with zero items is a card still being built, which is a different report — its embed list is what its own run needs, not a finding against it.

## A held slot is the hop to audit the next post, and an empty lane means you can submit into it

`backlog.py next` answering `wait — slot held by post <n> round <k>` blocks a *card*, not the renderer. Those are two different resources and a hop that reads only the first wastes the second:

- **The prompt audit needs no slot.** Read the next post in queue order, pull its live records' `## Prompt`, and the three triggers settle it before a pixel: `color_palette`, a literal mark, a text-inviting prop (a screen, a dial, a clock, a label). Write the rewrites, `lint` them on m1max and run `check-bboxes.py`; that is most of a batch's work and it is all reversible.
- **The render lane is the constraint, so read it before deciding.** If `imagegen status` on the running job reports a deep `queue_ahead`, the lane is busy and a new batch only pushes someone else's card later. If the lane is empty because the held post's renders have all finished and its card is with Wojtek, the Max is idle for the two hours he spends reviewing — submitting into it costs nobody anything and puts the next post's renders on disk before his next sitting. Say which of the two states you saw, because "the slot is held" alone does not justify either choice.
- **Count the cards already waiting on him before filling that idle lane.** An empty lane is only half the answer; the review queue is the other half, and it is what his attention actually costs. A night of batches can end with three cards open and not one answered, and a fourth post's six renders at that moment buy nothing — they land behind cards he has not opened. At two or three cards deep with every item pending, write the next post's brief and its prompts instead, leave the lane idle, and say in the comment that it is idle on purpose. Resume submitting on the wake where a card comes back answered. **Write the batch into that post's queue note before the run ends** — the prompt file stems for both engines, the lint and bbox results, the ship targets, and the words *written, not submitted* — because the next run's audit starts from the live records and will rewrite prompts that are already on disk and linted. The note is the only thing that says the work exists; a batch left unrecorded gets audited twice.
- **`backlog.py ready` is a claim that the renders are checked, so do not leave one queued against jobs still pending.** `batch-open-ready` pops ready entries regardless of the slot, and a sibling runner that takes it builds a card with nothing in staging. If the batch is submitted but not yet landed, the ready entry is a placeholder: say so in the post's `note` with the job ids and the collect time, so the run that reads it submits or waits rather than opening an empty card. The clean order is submit → monitor at the collect → fetch and check → `ready` → `batch-open-ready` in the collect run. Submitting early into a free lane is still the better trade, **but it does not get a ready entry**: a placeholder written in words is not a control and a sibling run pops it and builds an empty card. Leave the post `todo` with the job ids and collect time in its note, and write the ready entry in the collect run — see *The placeholder `ready` entry fires as a fault*.

## Count limbs on one whole-torso crop; a tight per-hand crop invents hands

Two crops of one frame disagreed about how many hands a figure had, and the tighter one was the liar. The scope it needs is the whole figure, torso to feet, at about 3x: on that crop the man had two arms, two hands, both closed fists, each attached where it should be. Crops taken around a hip only — 5x on 200 px — reported a third hand emerging "from under the waistband", because the trouser fold beside the second fist reads as a hand when the arm that belongs to it is outside the frame.

So split the two questions. **Counting is a whole-figure question**: one crop, roughly 40% of the frame width centred on the figure, 3x, asking for the count and for each hand's approximate position across the crop. **Shape is a per-hand question**: 5x–6x on a fist to see whether it has a thumb and countable knuckles. A per-hand crop never answers a count, and a count read on one hand-shaped region manufactures limbs.

The same trap runs the other way on a genuinely duplicated limb: the header of this batch really does have an extra arm, and the evidence that settled it was two *independent* crops, each framing the shoulder down to the hand on the same side, both showing an arm ending in a fused clump behind the working one. Agreement between two crops framed from different offsets is the signal; two tight crops of one hip is not.

## Match the crop's scope to the question's scope

The counting rule above, generalised. A crop answers a question only when its scope matches the question's, and both directions of mismatch produce a confident wrong answer:

- **A property of the whole object read off a part crop invents a small deviation.** A 3x crop of only the failed end of a shelf answered `slightly tilted, not hanging`, while a full-width crop of the same shelf showed the jar bases at the broken end sitting plainly lower than at the far end, about 10–15 degrees of droop. The narrow crop had no far end in it to measure against. Tilt, level, symmetry, proportion and how far a part has moved are **whole-object** questions: cut one crop containing the entire object, from its own extent (the full span of the shelf, the full width of the row).
- **A junction read off a full frame invents a plausible continuation.** A full-frame read answered that a rail on a bench `implies a continuous closed loop`; a 3x crop of the near end showed the two rails running straight and stopping behind the crank post, off the bench. At card size a rail disappearing behind a solid object looks like a rail going on, and the return that the section needs is then not in the picture. **Does it close, does it join, does it touch, does it hold** are junction questions: cut that junction alone at 3x or more.
- **Never ask either as a yes/no.** `Is the loop closed?` came back `yes, it implies a closed loop` from a frame whose closure was a two-pixel junction behind a post; the same question answered positionally — where each end of the track stops, in the frame's own words — reports the truth. This is the mechanism the direction check uses: a yes/no question hands the model the answer, so ask for a positional description and read the coordinates.
- **A count two crops dispute is settled on the widest crop of the row alone, then written in his terms.** A shelf of jars read 6 on one crop and 7 on two wider ones; recount on the crop that holds the whole row and use that number, and where two wide crops still disagree write it qualitatively in the item description (`more than the prompt's five`) rather than pinning a numeral under dispute. The beat is that the shelf is loaded, not the number.
- **A relation between two sizes is a whole-frame measurement, and a crop holding both objects understates it.** When the beat is "this thing is plainly wider than the opening it has to go through", the answer is two pixel extents, and a crop answers it at whatever magnification it flattened the pair: a 3x crop of an envelope lying under a posting slot read `approximately as wide as the opening, may be slightly wider`, while the full frame measured **450 px of envelope against a 150 px slot** — a three-to-one refusal the crop had made look like a near miss. Do not ask a reader to compare two widths; lay a grid on the full frame at a known pitch (`PIL` `ImageDraw.line` every 100 px in a saturated colour), then ask for the **left and right x of each object in those grid pixels** and subtract. Cross-check the ratio against the prompt's own bboxes before it reaches a card: numbers that agree with the prompt are a pass with both widths quoted in the item description, and the same numbers reversed are the hold-back a crop would have approved. The same treatment settles any size-relation beat — a prop too tall for a shelf, a crate wider than a hatch — where a yes/no question returns the answer the question offered.
- **A sameness beat ("the same jar twice, only the finish differs") is a ratio with a tolerance, and threshold segmentation cannot answer it.** Masking the two objects by luminance and saturation to get their bounding boxes reads every cast shadow and every tone of the wall behind them as part of the object: one pair of jars came back with a left box spanning the full frame height and heights of 652 against 582 px, contradicting the reader and each other. Do not build a silhouette by threshold — cut one crop holding both objects at roughly 1:1 and ask for an explicit ratio (`right jar height / left jar height`), then decide on the tolerance. **A few percent is not a defect for a state-contrast beat**: 5–6% taller and a few percent broader still says the same vessel in a new coat, so it ships with the number in the item description (`the glazed jar reads about a sixth taller; same profile, same mouth`), while a pair that reads as two different vessels — a different shoulder, a different neck — is the hold. Keep the count and the size as two separate questions: `exactly two jars, the paint tin between them is not one` is a whole-row crop at 1.3x, and it is what clause 4 asks about.
- **A count of openings is a whole-surface question, and a crop of the opening cannot answer it.** A beat of the form "this wall has exactly one opening in it" is settled by counting every opening across the entire surface, never by inspecting the one you asked for: an opening framed alone always contains precisely the window the prompt described, while the same wall cut edge to edge at about 1.3x showed a small near-square window at one end and a wide horizontal one at the other. Cut the surface band — the wall, the hull, the door leaf — from edge to edge of the frame, count the openings, and decide on that count. Expect the count to drift on one engine even when the prompt bans the second opening by name ("the one thing set into this wall, no door, no grille, no second opening anywhere" drew two lit windows on the ideogram and one on the flux), so check both engines of the slot before writing a repair and ship the engine that drew the count with the other one's failure named in his terms (`two lit windows where the beat is exactly one`).

## The lane is idle or it is not: one query, before any submit

Before writing a six-slot batch, check whether the Max is actually working, because "the slot is held" says nothing about the renderer. The fastest answer is the job table itself:

```bash
ssh m1max 'cd ~/git/ideogram4-local && date -u; sqlite3 jobs.db "select status,count(*) from jobs group by status"; sqlite3 jobs.db "select id,engine,created_at,finished_at from jobs order by created_at desc limit 3"'
```

Only `done` and `failed` rows, and a newest `finished_at` hours old, means the lane is empty: a batch submitted now runs from the front, keeps its ETA, and starves nobody. That is the one window in which a held slot costs nothing, and it is worth more than an hour of picking at an audit — a batch submitted behind someone else's night lands after the review it was meant for.

## The measured flux length ceiling is about 425 words, not 300

A flux twin carries the drawn-ink clause, the grain and the full negative clause in one prompt, and the old guideline of about 300 words made that impossible without cutting the clause that keeps lettering out. **Renders of 368 to 425 words came back with every clause intact and passed.** Above 425 is unverified rather than known-bad — do not claim a number there failed — but the negative clause is the first thing the encoder drops, so treat 425 as the working ceiling: a fresh twin written from a new composition usually lands 430–480 and needs about 40 words cut. The cuts come from merging the beat sentence into the corners sentence and from dropping repeated framing phrases ("filling the frame edge to edge" twice, "the same old" before an object already named). Never cut the negative clause to make the number.

**A twin assembled by concatenating the JSON element descriptions is not a twin.** Paste each element's `desc` into the flux prompt in order, behind the ink clause, and it runs 590–630 words — a third over the ceiling — because those descriptions were written for the structured engine and each one restates the framing, the palette and the cast. Write the twin as prose instead: the beat sentence first, then one sentence per object, the corners merged into the beat, the negative clause last. Budget the words as you write them rather than trimming after, and expect to rewrite a mechanically generated twin from scratch rather than cutting it down.

## The placeholder `ready` entry fires as a fault, and the collect run can repair the empty card

Marking a post `ready` before its renders land, so a sibling run knows the batch exists, is a real hazard and it has cost a rebuild. A sibling run read such an entry twelve minutes into a six-render batch, opened a card from the brief, and left it `in_progress` with **zero attachments and zero verdict interactions** for fifty minutes — a card he can open with nothing to judge on it. The note said "PLACEHOLDER, do not open" in words, and words are not a control.

Two rules follow. **Do not create the ready entry until there is something in staging**: write the submission brief, submit, and leave the post's note naming the job ids and the collect time; `ready` is for a round whose images exist. And when an empty card is already open, **the collecting run repairs it instead of opening a second one**: `checkoutRunId: null` plus no interactions is the signature, and an upload from the collecting run lands fine (`POST /companies/<cid>/issues/<id>/attachments`, `201` with `sha256`/`byteSize` to compare against staging), a linking comment with `attachmentIds` makes the PNGs visible in the thread, and `POST /issues/<id>/interactions` with `kind: request_item_verdicts` adds the verdict request. Then `PATCH` the description to the real card brief and set the issue `in_review`. One card per round still holds; the repair is the card, not a replacement for it.

**When the queue opens a slot with no issue at all (`backlog.py next` answering `create` with `slot.issue: null`), the collecting run is the one that builds the card from nothing, and the order is fixed.** Create the issue first — `POST /companies/<cid>/issues` with `title`, `description` (the card brief), `status: in_progress`, `priority`, `projectId` and `assigneeAgentId`, all carrying the run-id header — then upload, link and request verdicts against the id that call returned, then `PATCH` the status to `in_review`. Record the card on the slot last: `python3 backlog.py opened <n> <identifier>`, which is a different subcommand from `batch-opened` (that one writes to a `batch` entry, and a slot opened by `next` has no entry to write). **Take the identifier from `issue.identifier`, because `issue.key` is `null` on a freshly created issue** and reading only `key` looks like the board never assigned one; the number appears as `GUL-<n>` and is what `opened`, the queue note and every comment link need.

## A verdict wake reads what the card's own run already did before it ships anything

By the time an approval wakes this run, the run that owned the card has often done the whole bookkeeping: each record carries its approved row with the right round number, `superseded_by` points at the approved job, the record's `asset:` already names the vault path, and the queue has advanced. Check that first, because `backlog.py card-done <n>` answering `the slot is not held by post <n>` is the signal that the post was already closed — not a failed call, and not a licence to run the closing steps twice.

What is still this run's to verify:

- **The bytes.** Hash every shipped vault file against the staging copy that was on the card (`shasum -a 256`, `stat -f%z`). An approval says which job the picture came from; only the hash says the right file went into the draft.
- **The alt text against the shipped render, not against the brief.** The line can have been written from the prompt, or carried over from the picture it replaces, and survive the swap: one approved header's alt read `at night, cold light from an open doorway` for a warm panelled room lit through that same doorway. Rewriting that one line is inside the runner's remit — it belongs to the approved embed — while the rest of the prose stays the post author's.
- **The record's `asset:` path against the draft's embed.** A slot that shipped on its companion engine has its record naming the companion's file, so a record still pointing at the staging stem or at the main engine's name is the mismatch to fix rather than the file.
- **The vault filename is the one the draft's embed already names, and a new item id does not override it.** When the post's line already reads `assets/<date>/<name>.png` and the brief names that same file, the shipped bytes go there. Naming the asset after the slot's item id (`status-<section>.png`) while the post still embeds `<section>.png` leaves the live embed pointing at the file just superseded, so the post ships the old picture and nothing raises an error — copy the bytes over the embedded path, rename the previous file to `<name>.superseded-<old job>.png` beside it, and hash the result. The item id names the file only for a slot with no embed yet.
- **A record written at card-open carries the shipping promise in future tense, so the close rewrites it.** "It ships to `…` once he approves" becomes the past-tense line naming the stored byte size, the hash and the superseded neighbour's filename, with the `![[…]]` embed added under the H1 and one Verdicts row whose `round` cell is the **card's** round (a round-2 card writes `| 2 |`), not the attempt suffix on the render. An approved record still saying where the file *will* go reads, to the next run, as a render that never shipped.
- **A render that appears in staging after the verdict does not replace the file he approved.** Another runner can submit a re-render of an already-approved slot — it lands minutes to hours after the card closed, with no card of its own and no verdict — and the temptation is to treat the newer frame as an improvement. Do not: an approval is a verdict on one picture, and swapping it for a frame he has never seen puts an unreviewed image in the post under his name. Keep the approved bytes, name the orphan in the queue note so it is not rediscovered as a mystery later, and say in the comment that the shipped file is the one he approved. A better frame that he wants is a new card, and that is his call, not a tidy-up.

## A vision tool's `region` argument is not a crop, and a 3x lump is a suspicion
Settle hand shape on a PNG you cut and upscale yourself, never on a vision tool's `region`
argument. `vision_analyze`'s region is `[x1, y1, x2, y2]` in original-image pixels, not the bbox
order `[y1, x1, y2, x2]` the prompt files use, so a box passed in bbox order crops a strip of
floor or wall — where the reader answers `no hands in this crop`, a confident non-answer that
reads exactly like a pass.

Aim the box from fractions of a wide figure crop, never from a reader's absolute pixel
coordinates: boxes asked for that way landed on a shelf, on a bench top and on bare wall, and each
one answered `no hand present`, which is evidence about the box and not about the hand.

Then read shape at 6x. A 3x read of two fists called both `fused lumps with no separated
fingers`; the same file at 6x showed a thumb and knuckle lines on each, and that render passed. A
genuine defect survives the same zoom — one header's hands read as fused lumps at 5x and again at
8x. So write `lump` off a 2x or 3x read as a suspicion only: confirm it at 5x–6x before it holds a
slot, and confirm a hold from two different crops before it goes on a card as a defect. **2x is the
more common false alarm of the two**, because at that scale a one-pixel ink gap between two drawn
fingers is all that separates a fist from a block: a flux render whose fists a 2x read called
`fused, simplified lumps` showed a thumb and knuckle lines on both hands at 5x.

## When the vision tool is down, the render waits — it does not get guessed at

`vision_analyze` fails with `Error analyzing: Connection error` for **every** image, of any size, when the service behind it is unavailable; it happened for a 2.7 MB render, a 1.1k-wide crop and a 600 px control JPEG in a row. Do not read a repeated connection error as "this image is bad", and do not fall back on a description you half-remember from a sibling frame: the edge scripts are mechanical and still useful, but they cannot tell a raised triangle from a dent, and clause 4 is a read.

The disposition is to **hold the render unread and re-arm the monitor** — a couple of minutes' wait is usually enough — then judge it on the next hop with the same crops. Say on the issue that the frame passes the scripts and is unread, so nobody treats silence as a pass. The only thing that must not happen is an unread frame on a card.

**A verdict taken while the tool was silent is provisional, and the first read after it comes back is the one that counts — re-cut the crop, do not carry the old finding forward.** A frame held during an outage on a single earlier read stays a suspicion until it is re-read on a differently framed crop once the tool answers. One ideogram was held as `a field of raised triangular blocks`; the 2x crop taken on the next wake showed the marks genuinely **cut into** the wood, splinters at their edges and grain running into each wedge — the object had been drawn, just tiled into dozens. That changed the repair completely: `the mark cannot be drawn` becomes `the mark is drawn too many times`, and the next attempt is one bounded mark rather than a new object. So write no prompt and open no author escalation off a finding from an outage; re-read first, then decide which repair it actually is.

## A card write that answers 400 or 422 is usually a cap, and the caps are in one file

An item description over 500 characters, a `monitor.notes` over 500, an unknown key inside an
`executionPolicy.monitor` block, or `monitorNextCheckAt` sent as a top-level PATCH field each
answer `400` — several of them with an **empty body** — and each failure takes the whole request
with it. A rejected verdict request leaves the card with no interaction at all, so the next
`in_review` PATCH answers `422 invalid_issue_disposition`: nothing owns the next action because
the card that was supposed to never landed.

The accepted payload shapes, the exact caps, the order a card is built in, and the one-call
diagnosis that settles any of it (`GET /api/openapi.json`, then walk
`paths['/api/issues/{id}']['patch']['requestBody']['content']['application/json']['schema']`) are
in [review-card-api.md](review-card-api.md). Read that file before hand-writing a card or a monitor
PATCH rather than after the second empty 400.

**When the arm answers 400 with an empty body, resend the same payload before changing it.** The
refusal is usually a race with the monitor's own trigger transition, not a bad field: the identical
bodies (minimal, `+scheduledBy`, `+notes`, `+maxAttempts`) all answered `200` a minute later, after
an arm of the same shape had been refused. The one field with a durable cause is `maxAttempts` on an
issue with a long monitor history — `40` was refused while `100` (the schema maximum) was accepted in
the same second — so drop `maxAttempts` from the call or send 100, and do not spend calls bisecting
`notes` and `scheduledBy`, which were never the problem.

**Furniture fused into the figure is a mangled figure, not occlusion.** A figure standing at or
behind a bench, table or railing can come back with the work surface joined into his body: the
torso ends at the bench top with no hips, and the legs and boots stand separately below it as if in
front. At full frame this reads as an ordinary figure behind a bench and passes a read — it is still
a hold-back, because the frame is then not one continuous scene and the bar's figure clause covers
it. Crop the waist at 2x on any figure with a work surface in front of him and ask whether the hips
join the torso behind the surface; a read that calls it normal occlusion is the reason to crop, not
the finding. In the prompt, state the figure's own continuity — hips and legs drawn behind the
bench, the bench edge passing in front of them — because the model is solving two boxes and will
join whatever it likes unless the join is described.

**A reused object class inverts the count.** "One panel locked, six open"
rendered as a **locker bank: seven shut doors, each with its own padlock**, because a locker is an
object whose default state is shut and locked — the prose count cannot beat the object's own
affordance. The beat read as the opposite of the section and the slot was held. When a beat is a
COUNT ("one of seven", "six of seven"), state the number in the objects and give each object its own
state: one padlocked door described alone on the wall, then six open doors described one by one with
a plug on the floor under each. Avoid giving the counter-example the same hardware as the example —
eight locks in a frame cannot say "exactly one lock".

**A gate is the same trap: its default state is shut, so an open gate has to be said twice and a shut one is drawn with the latch engaged.** A header whose whole beat was "the stop was set level with the part it should have stopped, so the gate can never catch" came back from one engine with the gate swung open and the latch free (right) and from the other with the gate **closed and latched** (the opposite: it says the lock works). Write the state in the object's own words — *swung back flat against the inner wall so the whole doorway stands open and dark, the gate not shut and not across the opening* — and accept that the engine that shuts it is answering the noun `gate` and not the sentence about it. **Saying it that way can overshoot: the same rewrite came back from that engine with the gate off its hinges and propped against the brick wall, no doorway around it and no iron on the jamb at all — a spare door leaning in a room, which is a third wrong picture.** So state the mounting as well as the state: the leaf *hangs on its two strap hinges from the jamb*, the doorway is *cut in the wall*, and the leaf swings back against that wall **on those hinges**. An object described only by where it has ended up loses the thing it was fixed to, and a prop detached from its frame reads as a different story, not as a sloppier drawing of this one.

**A beat that needs two parts level needs both parts as their own element.** A header asked for a latch hanging from its pivot and a catch-plate bolted to the jamb at
*exactly the same height*, because "the stop was set level with the thing it should have stopped" is
the section. The two pieces were written in one element and the ideogram drew one of them: the gate
came back wide open with the latch swinging free and **the jamb beside it bare brick, no plate at
all**. An open gate says "nobody locked it", which is a different story from "the lock was set
wrong", so the slot was held. Any beat whose meaning is a RELATIONSHIP between two objects (level
with, shorter than, above, clear of) wants each object in its own element with its own box, and the
relationship restated in both descriptions. Folding them into one element buys a sixth of the
element budget and loses the beat.

**A wide description box for one figure draws two figures.** A version-tag ideogram in the same batch put an older bearded man and a younger boy standing side by side. The prompt said "exactly one
person only, seen once in the picture, no second figure", and the man's box was `x 380-700` — nearly
half the frame width and a full figure tall. Two people fit comfortably inside that box, so the
engine put two in. Narrowing the box to a single column (`x 290-520`) and adding "no child, no boy,
no reflection of him" plus "the rest of the floor is bare flags with nobody on it" is the fix. The
lesson generalises: figure duplication is not only a wording problem, it is a BOX AREA problem —
give a lone figure a box the width of one person, and say what occupies the rest of the floor.

**`check-bboxes.py` validates each description against its own box and nothing else, so read the printed table for overlaps yourself.** Two failures it cannot see, in one set of five elements: a chest whose box sat above the workbench's top line, so it floated over the bench with its base in mid-air; and that same box overlapping the figure's box, which is where the engine merges two objects into one shape. Neither contradicts the element's own desc, so the script passes and the render comes back with the chest joined to the man. After the script, walk the table as geometry: every object that stands on a surface must overlap that surface's box at its base and must not share area with the figure's box. Move the box, cut the element, or give the object its own described support — the fix belongs in the layout, not in the wording.

**Aim a waist crop by the figure, never by the prompt's box.** Wide crops invent fusion: a 2.2x cut
spanning half the frame width reported a header figure "cut through the midsection, torso merged into
the bench" on a picture whose tight 3x crop of the midsection shows the shirt hem, the belt, the hips
and both legs down to the boots with the bench standing behind him — the bench's front edge crossing
the body plane is what the wide read called a cut. A wide strip of floor with a hard shadow edge does
the same and reads as a gap under the shoes. Keep the crop to roughly one body width and treat any
wide-crop "fused body" as a suspicion a tight crop has to settle before it costs a slot.

**A clearance beat is measured on the object's own bottom edge, and a cast shadow reads as contact at 3x.**
When the section's meaning IS the gap — the load came off the floor, the crate is lifted, the envelope
hangs above the gate — the gap cannot be settled on a wide frame read or on a 3x crop. One suspended
crate passed a full-frame read as `hanging clear with a shadow beneath it`, then a 3x crop of the same
area as `resting on the tiles`, because its cast shadow runs parallel to the bottom plank about a
plank-gap under it and the shadow's hard edge reads as the contact line. Two crops of the bottom edge
alone, at 5x and 6x, showed the lit strip of tile between plank and shadow: the object was clear.
So cut the object's bottom edge by itself at 5x–6x and require an **unshadowed lit strip between the
object and its shadow**; a gap one quarter of the plank's height is real but reads as marginal, which
is a caveat for the card (`the air gap is narrow — a lit strip of tile, not a hand's width`), not a
hold-back, provided the sibling engine of the slot draws the clearance plainly.

## When a slot has outlived three objects, the post's author picks the fourth

A slot that has been through **three prompt objects** — each one a different physical thing, not a rewording — and roughly seven renders across both engines has stopped being a prompt problem, and continuing to guess at it spends a render every round on the same question. The ceiling is real and worth writing down, because the temptation at four failures is to try a fifth object that is only slightly different from the fourth.

Hand the slot to the post's author instead of inventing again:

- **Open a child issue assigned to the author**, parented to the campaign issue, `status: todo`, `priority: medium`. `POST /companies/<cid>/issues` with `title`, `description`, `parentId`, `projectId`, `assigneeAgentId` and the run-id header; `issue.identifier` is the `GUL-<n>` to quote on the campaign issue.
- **The description carries, in this order:** the post path and the slot's embed line; the section the picture sits above, quoted in one line so the author knows which claim it illustrates; every object tried with **engine by engine** what each render did (this is what stops him proposing the same object again); the bar in the owner's own terms — no lettering, no screens or dials, no second figure or animal, no literal marks, nothing that wants writing on, one continuous painted scene; and **one or two proven alternatives from the campaign that he may simply pick** (`use the bell from the header two posts back` is a good answer, and it is cheaper than a new idea).
- **The card does not wait for the answer.** A slot with an escalation behind it goes up on the existing card as **deliberately absent**, named in `detailsMarkdown` as absent with the reason, and the child issue id quoted. Holding three clean images for a picture that does not exist yet costs the owner the afternoon.
- **Say on the campaign issue that the slot is escalated and is not stalled**, with the child issue id, so a reviewer reading four open cards does not read this one as a run that stopped halfway.
- **One attempt may still be owed on the current object.** When the failure was a *shape* the engine keeps rounding up — a mark that tiles, a count that drifts — the single-mark or edge-sited variant is worth one render **in a later round with an idle lane**, not in the round that escalates, and the comment says plainly that the author's answer overrides it. Do not spend that render while the question is open and the queue is loaded.

### When the answer comes back

- **The delivery path is the child issue finishing: the parent wakes as `issue_children_completed`.** A card cannot be handed back to the run that opened it (Paperclip refuses it as a delegation cycle), so nothing else will announce the answer — read the child on that wake, and read its **document** as well as its comment (`GET /issues/<id>/documents`): the one-line answer usually links a full spec that carries the composition, the prompt and the reject checks.
- **The author gives the composition and the count; you keep the register.** Take his objects, his numbers, his one action and his reject checks as written, and translate the prose into the series' style block — if his draft asks for `warm oil-on-board illustration` while the post's other images are 1990s anime cel with film grain, the header ships in cel or it will not sit with its own post. Say in the comment that the medium is the one thing you changed and why, so the change reads as a decision rather than as a quiet rewrite.
- **His answer usually contains the diagnosis, and the diagnosis is worth more than the object.** The one that closed this slot — *carry the wrongness with an object in an unmistakable pose plus a calm room, never with an absence and never with a mark* — went into `prompt-hygiene.md` and shapes every later beat of that shape. Lift the sentence into the skill, not just into the prompt.
- **His reject checks become a numbered list on the brief, run at the crop.** Eight binary questions (clapper clear of the rim, two bolts entering the brick, no letter or numeral anywhere, mug upright and steaming, steam not smoke, bell not red, no second bell or figure or phone or cable, bell not dead centre) catch what the edge scripts cannot, and they are the author's terms rather than yours.
- **A named fallback replaces a new idea, it does not join it.** When he writes *if this fails twice, use the proven composition verbatim*, that is the next step and there is no fifth object to invent — record the fallback on the brief and in the monitor note so the collect run cannot reach for a new one.
- **His instruction to delete the replaced file does not delete it.** An author asking for the broken September asset to be removed is asking you not to put it back on a card; the vault keeps every file, so it becomes `<slot>.superseded-<old job>.png` beside its replacement when the new render is approved, and the rename is stated in the comment. Offer the deletion as the owner's call rather than performing it.

## The engine repeats a limb rather than drawing an end to it, so take the limb out of the frame
spare leg and a spare arm fail the same way, and both survive a naive count: a boot-level crop
reports two boots while a third trouser leg hangs between the real pair and ends in mid-air,
bootless. The counting method for that is in [counting-checks.md](counting-checks.md); the decision
belongs here. When a limb has failed a slot, do not re-describe it more precisely — restructure so it
is not in the frame: cut the figure at the waist behind the bench, rest both fists on a described
surface, or take him out of the picture entirely and let the props carry the beat.
